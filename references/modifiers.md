# Modifiers, Placement, and Reference

Covers where modifiers go and what they point at: prepositional-phrase and participle placement, danglers, list/parallel structure, relative clauses (overload, commas, whittling), pronoun antecedents, and which adverbs earn their keep. Governing standard: the Reader attaches every modifier to the closest possible word and expects every pronoun and list item to resolve instantly.
Not covered here: general fat-cutting rationale (concision.md) and subordination of main news (structure.md).
Examples note: BEFORE/AFTER pairs model a writer revising their own text; when editing someone else's, any AFTER that adds facts requires source support or an `[AUTHOR CHECK]` (SKILL.md invariant).

### Nearest-Word Attachment
**Rule:** Place every prepositional phrase and modifier immediately adjacent to the word it modifies.
**Detect:** PP (with/in/on/for/by/at/of + noun) whose neighbor is not its logical target; another noun intervenes between phrase and target; sentence-final PP sitting after an object it doesn't describe ("announced it's going to snow on the news").
**Fix:**
1. Identify the word the phrase logically modifies (verb, noun, pronoun, adjective).
2. Move the phrase directly beside that word; front it if needed.
3. Reread — confirm no new false attachment appeared.
4. If every position reads oddly, escalate to Rewrite the Modifier Pileup.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: They announced it's going to snow on the news. AFTER: They announced on the news that it's going to snow.
- BEFORE: I photographed a moose in my bathrobe. AFTER: In my bathrobe, I photographed a moose. (Fronting attaches the phrase to "I," not the moose.)
- BEFORE: Gourmet meals expertly delivered by couriers in elegant packaging. AFTER: Gourmet meals in elegant packaging expertly delivered by couriers.
**Exception:** If the phrase already abuts its true target and no competing noun sits closer, leave it. The test is Reader expectation, not mechanical adjacency.

### Un-Dangle Participles and Fronted Modifiers
**Rule:** Ensure every participial phrase sits beside the noun it modifies; if a fronted participle points at the wrong noun, make the true doer the subject or convert the participle to a subordinate clause.
**Detect:** Sentence-initial -ing/-ed/-en phrase + comma where the main-clause subject is not the doer — especially when the subject is a body part, inanimate object, or a different person than the doer.
**Fix:**
1. Identify the true doer of the participial action.
2. Rewrite so that noun is the subject, immediately after the participial phrase; or
3. Convert the participle to a subordinate clause with an explicit subject ("While I was walking..."), which frees it from adjacency; or
4. Move the modifier next to the noun it actually describes.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Daydreaming about Rosa, Theo's foot landed in a puddle. AFTER: Daydreaming about Rosa, Theo stepped in a puddle.
- BEFORE: Walking through the market, my arms got tired. AFTER: While I was walking through the market, my arms got tired.
- BEFORE: Glazed with honey, Marco served the ham. AFTER: Marco served the ham, glazed with honey.
**Exception:** A fronted participle modifying the actual subject is fine ("Exhausted, Harry fell into bed"). Don't fret whether the unit is a "phrase" or "clause" — only placement matters.

### Possessives Can't Anchor Modifiers
**Rule:** Attach a fronted modifier to the headword of the following noun phrase, never to a possessive inside it — a possessive is itself a modifier and cannot receive modification.
**Detect:** Fronted appositive or participle + noun phrase shaped "X's Y" where the modifier describes X but grammatically hits headword Y. The apostrophe-s on the apparent target is the giveaway.
**Fix:**
1. Find the headword of the noun phrase after the modifier.
2. Check whether the modifier actually describes the possessive noun instead.
3. If so, recast so the described entity appears as a plain noun the modifier can abut, or move the description into the predicate.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: A Wimbledon-winning champion, Serena's coach was proud. AFTER: Serena's coach was proud of the Wimbledon-winning champion.
- BEFORE: A best-selling novelist, Elena's agent booked the tour. AFTER: The agent of Elena, a best-selling novelist, booked the tour.
**Exception:** If the modifier genuinely describes the headword ("A veteran negotiator, Elena's agent..."), the structure is correct.

### Resolve Ambiguous Third-Person Pronouns
**Rule:** When a third-person pronoun could refer to more than one preceding noun, replace it with the specific noun — then trust later pronouns until a competing candidate intervenes. "Repetitiveness is better than chaos."
**Detect:** he/him/his, she/her/hers, it/its, they/them/their with two or more gender-and-number-matching noun phrases in reach. Classic shape: compound subject joined by "and" followed by a singular pronoun ("the detective and the smuggler ... his heart"). On every reread pass, audit third-person forms and possessive determiners; first-person forms rarely need scrutiny.
**Fix:**
1. List every preceding noun phrase matching the pronoun's gender and number.
2. If exactly one candidate, leave the pronoun alone.
3. If more, substitute the intended noun — or a synonym/identifying detail unique to the referent ("dropping his stolen saddlebags").
4. If no graceful alternative exists, repeat the noun exactly, even if it just appeared.
5. Walk forward: keep later pronouns for the established referent unless intervening text reintroduces a competitor — then name the noun again.
6. Terminal state: when nothing in the text resolves which candidate is meant (the ambiguity is the author's, not the prose's), keep the sentence and emit an `[AUTHOR CHECK]` that enumerates the candidate readings ("Bob's department or Jim's?") so the author can answer in one word. Never pick a referent by guess.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: As the detective and the smuggler fired, a bullet pierced his heart. AFTER: As the detective and the smuggler fired, a bullet pierced the smuggler's heart.
- BEFORE: The smuggler fell. The smuggler was dead. AFTER: He fell to the ground. He was dead. (Once named, the pronoun is clear — don't over-replace.)
- BEFORE: The CEO praised the CFO before she resigned. AFTER: The CEO praised the CFO before the CFO resigned.
**Exception:** A pronoun with a single matching candidate needs no change ("Marta lost her car keys"). First-person pronouns carry little risk.

### Give Vague "It," "That," and "Which" a Stated Antecedent
**Rule:** When it/that/which points at a verb, clause, or whole situation rather than a stated noun, recast with a gerund or explicit noun phrase as the referent.
**Detect:** "It is/was why/how/because..." where no preceding singular noun survives substitution (the antecedent is a verb or idea); sentence-opening "That's / This is" after a sentence packing multiple actions; trailing ", which" after several ideas. Test: list all candidate referents — if more than one fits, or only a verb fits, rewrite.
**Fix:**
1. Substitute each candidate noun for the pronoun to test fit.
2. If none fits, name the action or idea it secretly means.
3. Rewrite with that idea explicit: a gerund ("Coding daily..."), a "The fact that..." clause, or a possessive noun phrase ("Priya's knowledge of statistics...").
4. Delete the orphaned pronoun; confirm only one reading survives.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Priya knows statistics. It is why she landed the internship. AFTER: Priya's knowledge of statistics is why she landed the internship.
- BEFORE: Though it rained, we caught most of the game. That's what life is all about. AFTER: ... Spending time with my son is what life is all about.
- BEFORE: We missed the train but found a tiny bakery, which made the whole trip. AFTER: ... found a tiny bakery. Discovering that bakery made the whole trip.
**Exception:** "It" pointing at a just-named concrete noun, or a summarizing "that" after a sentence with only one plausible referent, reads instantly — leave it.

### One List, One Form (Stem-Distribution Test)
**Rule:** Make every item in a coordinated list take the same grammatical form and attach to the shared stem the same way; the distributed combination must be both grammatical and true.
**Detect:** Series where one conjunct's head differs (a finite verb phrase among noun objects, a PP among clauses, a bare adverb between verb phrases). Mechanical test: concatenate stem + each item and parse — flag failures like "runs lasts long" or "relax in by the pool." Also flag semantic clashes: a stem frequency/scope word (daily, hourly, always, every) contradicting an item's own time word ("open daily for ... Sunday supper").
**Fix:**
1. Split the series into items; label each item's form.
2. Run stem + item for every item; mark ungrammatical or false combinations.
3. Equalize: add the missing verb/preposition/article to deviant items until each is a structural equal; or
4. Close the list: end it with the last same-form item, then move mismatched material into its own clause with its own subject or verb (", and it..." / ", and she...").
5. Move any item contradicting a stem modifier outside that modifier's scope ("and on Sundays for supper").
6. Re-run the test until every combination passes.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Marta has visited Kenya, Peru, loves Portugal, and Iceland. AFTER: Marta has visited Kenya, Peru, and Iceland, and she loves Portugal.
- BEFORE: This laptop boots fast, quietly, lasts long, and holds a charge. AFTER: This laptop boots fast, boots quietly, lasts long, and holds a charge. (Equalizing: repetition beats a broken list.)
- BEFORE: Relax in the lobby, the courtyard, or by the fountain. AFTER: Relax in the lobby, in the courtyard, or by the fountain.
- BEFORE: Rosa's Diner is open daily for breakfast and lunch and Sunday supper. AFTER: Rosa's Diner is open daily for breakfast and lunch, and on Sundays for supper.
**Exception:** "Every parallel poses its own unique dangers" — no formula covers all cases. Judge by whether the Reader stumbles, not by mechanical symmetry.

### Comma-Mark Restrictive vs. Nonrestrictive Relative Clauses
**Rule:** Set off a relative clause with commas only if it is nonrestrictive (removable without harming the main clause's meaning); give a restrictive, defining clause no commas.
**Detect:** Removal test — delete the clause. If the main clause becomes untrue or over-broad ("Any car must be electric"), the clause is restrictive: no commas. If the main clause stands alone, it is nonrestrictive: commas required. Scan for mismatches both ways.
**Fix:**
1. Remove the clause mentally; reread the main clause.
2. Meaning harmed or broadened → restrictive → strip surrounding commas.
3. Meaning intact → nonrestrictive → enclose in commas.
4. Verify the punctuated sentence names the group/thing you actually mean.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The bonus will go to the interns, who finished early. AFTER: The bonus will go to the interns who finished early. (Comma removal restricts the bonus to early finishers only.)
- BEFORE: Any car, that I lease, must be electric. AFTER: Any car that I lease must be electric.
- BEFORE: The bridge which has rust is closed. AFTER: The bridge, which has rust, is closed. (Extra info about an already-identified bridge.)
**Exception:** None — this distinction is meaning-bearing, not stylistic.

### One Relative Clause Per Sentence — Extra Light, Not Backstory
**Rule:** Keep at most one relative clause per sentence, and use it only to cast extra light on its noun; promote history, backstory, or "oh, by the way" facts to their own sentences or cut them.
**Detect:** Two or more that/which/who/whom (or zero-relative) clauses in one sentence; nesting-doll chains (which...which...who); long postmodifier stretches separating subject from verb; a clause carrying dates, founding events, or biography peripheral to the main claim (test: prefix it with "oh, by the way" — if sense is unchanged, it's smuggled backstory).
**Fix:**
1. Mark every relative clause and its target noun; identify the main clause's point.
2. Keep only the clause that most directly serves that point.
3. Promote each remaining clause to its own sentence, converting the relative pronoun to a repeated noun or personal pronoun.
4. Delete clauses that are pure minutiae.
5. Confirm each resulting sentence carries at most one relative clause.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The new menu that debuts next spring and that is Chef Rivera's brainchild, who opened the bistro in 1998, will shorten Sunday lines. AFTER: The new menu is the brainchild of Chef Rivera, who opened the bistro in 1998. It will shorten Sunday lines.
- BEFORE: The bakery, which my grandmother opened in 1962 after emigrating from Naples, raised its prices. AFTER: My grandmother opened the bakery in 1962 after emigrating from Naples. This week it raised its prices.
- BEFORE: The earlier ferry will ease crowding on the last sailing, which is traditionally the fullest. AFTER: (unchanged — one clause casting extra light; leave alone.)
**Exception:** Fiction: a skilled writer may deliberately stack clauses for rhythm or irony (Gaiman does). It's a choice, not a law — know you have it.

### Rewrite the Modifier Pileup
**Rule:** When moving a prepositional phrase would only park it against a different wrong word, rewrite: cut modifiers already implied, split into two sentences, or front a modifier with a colon.
**Detect:** A noun phrase trailing two or more stacked modifiers (participial + prepositional) where every reordering still leaves some phrase beside a word it doesn't modify. Common in ad copy, headlines, and other verbless constructions.
**Fix:**
1. Try the simple move first (Nearest-Word Attachment).
2. If every arrangement reads oddly, delete any modifier obvious from context.
3. Otherwise split the sentence, giving one modifier its own short sentence.
4. Or pull one modifier to the front, set off with a colon.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Blender set designed to delight chefs with steel blades for fast crushing. AFTER: Blender set with steel blades for fast crushing. Chefs love it.
- BEFORE: Knife set designed to please cooks with carbon edge for clean slicing. AFTER: Designed to please cooks: knife set with carbon edge for clean slicing.
**Exception:** Prefer the simple move whenever it works. Whether a survivable-but-weird sentence must be rebuilt is a judgment call about Reader comfort.

### Check Trailing-Modifier Scope Over Lists
**Rule:** When a modifier trails a comma-separated list, verify whether the Reader reads it as covering all items or only the last; restructure if both readings are plausible.
**Detect:** Shape "A, B, and C with X." Test both readings. Flag when both parse plausibly; pass when world knowledge forces one (whipped cream goes on strawberries; gusto covers all three songs).
**Fix:**
1. Decide intended scope: whole list or last item only.
2. Whole list → move the modifier before the list (usually front the sentence).
3. Last item only → reorder the list or bind the modifier tighter ("strawberries topped with whipped cream").
4. Reread — only one reading should survive.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: He photographed the barn, the fence, and the horses in the fog. AFTER: In the fog, he photographed the barn, the fence, and the horses.
- BEFORE: Dana ate ravioli, pizza, and strawberries with whipped cream. AFTER: (unchanged — world knowledge attaches the cream to strawberries only.)
**Exception:** Readers resolve most list-final modifiers effortlessly. Intervene only when a genuine misreading is plausible.

### Deletion-Test Every Manner Adverb
**Rule:** Target only manner and degree adverbs (never time, place, sentence, or conjunctive adverbs); delete each unless the sentence is worse without it, with no redundancy and no "look at me" showiness. This deletion test is the single authority on adverb keep/cut decisions — the trigger lists in `concision.md` only generate candidates; the test decides.
**Detect:** In-scope: -ly adverbs on action verbs (walked quickly), degree words (very, really, truly, absolutely). Out-of-scope — never cut on this rule's authority: when-words (soon, tomorrow, now), where-words (there, outside), sentence-initial linkers + comma (however, consequently), sentence commentary (frankly, tragically). Cut signals: adverb entailed by verb/tense/context (shouted loudly; has previously written — redundancy details in concision.md); stacked adverbs ("wildly and furiously"); loud intensifiers on already-vivid verbs or dialogue tags (purred seductively) — "the loudest person in the room is the weakest person in the room." Keep signals: changes the verb's meaning (laughed cruelly), adds pace/urgency nothing else carries (immediately), characterizes delivery (said simply).
**Fix:**
1. Classify each candidate by the question it answers (when/where/manner/degree/whole-sentence); pass only manner/degree forward. Check a dictionary for ambiguous words (well, fast, tomorrow).
2. Write the clause without the adverb; compare.
3. Identical or better without it → delete; do not substitute a synonym.
4. Stacked or intensifying adverbs in dramatic passages and dialogue tags → delete all of them; resist re-adding intensity. (Sharpest in fiction, where understatement carries force.)
5. Keep only if all three hold: real added information (ask: does the surrounding context already establish this manner?), no redundancy, no showiness.
6. In LIGHT EDIT, a manner adverb retained on a voice judgment call — while its siblings were cut — gets a one-line note or `[AUTHOR CHECK]` naming the keep decision, so retention is a ruling, not an oversight.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Victor menacingly gave Hugo a sneer, then wildly and furiously began striding toward him. AFTER: Victor gave Hugo a sneer, then began striding toward him.
- BEFORE: "Come closer," Marlene purred seductively. AFTER: "Come closer," Marlene purred. (The verb already conveys it; leave something to the imagination.)
- BEFORE: We'll meet tomorrow. Leave the boxes outside. AFTER: (unchanged — tomorrow and outside answer when and where; not targets.)
- BEFORE: Senator Vane laughed. AFTER: Senator Vane laughed cruelly. (Keeper: the adverb changes the meaning of the laugh.)
**Exception:** The rule is a tool, not a law: manner adverbs that pass the test stay ("Penny left quickly"; "Clean this up immediately"). Sentence and conjunctive adverbs (Tragically,; However,) add commentary and linkage — never purge them.

### Show Results, Don't Tell Manner
**Rule:** Replace an adverb that tells what an action was like with concrete detail showing the action's observable result.
**Detect:** Vivid action verb + manner adverb of force, intensity, or evaluation (slammed forcefully, struck violently, gripped tightly) where a physical consequence could be depicted instead.
**Fix:**
1. Delete the adverb.
2. Ask what visible result the manner implies (what broke, moved, changed).
3. If a result strengthens the sentence, append it as a participial phrase or short clause.
4. If no result is worth showing, leave the bare verb.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Dana slammed the laptop shut forcefully. AFTER: Dana slammed the laptop shut, cracking the screen.
- BEFORE: Marco gripped the railing tightly. AFTER: Marco gripped the railing, his knuckles whitening.
**Exception:** Showing is optional: sometimes the bare verb is the fix, and a plain telling adverb can survive the deletion test when brevity beats depiction.

### Disambiguate Gerund/Participle -ing Words
**Rule:** When an -ing word before a noun reads as either a gerund (an action) or a participial adjective (describing the noun), restructure — often by inserting "that" — so only one reading survives.
**Detect:** -ing + noun where both readings parse (visiting relatives, moving vans); trigger words: visiting, leading, moving, flying. Flag "the trends leading experts have predicted..." shapes where a missing "that" lets the -ing word masquerade as the sentence's verb.
**Fix:**
1. Decide the intended reading: action or description.
2. Gerund reading → recast explicitly: "It can be fun to visit relatives."
3. Adjective reading inside a relative clause → insert "that" after the head noun: "the colors that leading designers predicted."
4. Or swap the ambiguous word for an unambiguous synonym ("top" for "leading").
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Visiting grandparents can be exhausting. AFTER: It can be exhausting to visit grandparents. (Or, for the other reading: Grandparents who visit can be exhausting.)
- BEFORE: Here are the colors leading designers have predicted will be hot. AFTER: Here are the colors that leading designers have predicted will be hot.
**Exception:** A sentence the Reader can parse after a pause isn't strictly wrong — it's "usually best" not to force a doubletake, but harmless ambiguity or rhythm can win.

### Complete Suspended Compounds
**Rule:** When list items lean on a shared tail word (players, designers, technicians), make every item attach to it the same way, or give each item its own complete head word.
**Detect:** Series ending in a plural head noun where earlier items are complete agent nouns but later items are bare attributive nouns: "..., NOUN, and NOUN HEAD-plural" (drummer, keyboard, and guitar players).
**Fix:**
1. Identify the shared tail noun.
2. For each item, ask: complete alone, or dependent on the tail?
3. If mixed, give every dependent item its own copy of the tail, or replace with complete forms (keyboardist, guitarist).
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Nina praised the crew's writer, editor, producer, lighting, and sound technicians. AFTER: Nina praised the crew's writer, editor, producer, lighting technician, and sound technician.
- BEFORE: The gala honored the film's director, composer, costume, and set designers. AFTER: The gala honored the film's director, composer, costume designer, and set designer.
**Exception:** A fully suspended compound whose items ALL share the tail ("keyboard and guitar players") is fine; the fault is mixing complete and incomplete items.

### Repeat the Shared Function Word
**Rule:** Repeat the shared preposition or function word before each list item whenever a single occurrence could make the Reader hesitate, even if one occurrence is technically grammatical.
**Detect:** One preposition (by, in, to, for, of) before the first item of a series whose items are long, dissimilar, or joined by "as well as" — especially "VERB-ed by X, as well as Y," where Y could momentarily read as a direct object.
**Fix:**
1. Locate the shared function word.
2. If any item is distant, lengthy, or joined by "as well as," insert the word before each subsequent item.
3. Reread: every item should attach instantly, no backtracking.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: I was captivated by the murals, as well as the string quartet. AFTER: I was captivated by the murals, as well as by the string quartet.
- BEFORE: She was cheered by the crowd and the band. AFTER: (unchanged — two short adjacent items; the single "by" distributes cleanly.)
**Exception:** The unrepeated form is often defensible; repeat only where the single word makes the Reader work.

### Elide Only What the Reader Instantly Restores
**Rule:** Leave a noun or predicate implied only when the implication is unmistakable; otherwise state the omitted words outright or simplify the wordy structure that forced the ellipsis.
**Detect:** Sentences ending "X is." / "X is, too." / "X was."; lists sharing an omitted head noun. Test: restore the omitted words from the prior sentence — if restoration requires copying a long phrase ("is among the fields that..."), the Reader will stumble.
**Fix:**
1. Reconstruct the full sentence with the omitted words restored.
2. Short and obvious → keep the ellipsis.
3. Otherwise write the omitted words outright, or
4. Rewrite the first sentence leaner so the ellipsis becomes trivial to fill.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Health care and education are among the fields expected to add jobs. The retail industry is. AFTER: Health care and education are expected to add jobs. Retail is, too.
- BEFORE: Dana is exhausted. Marcus is, too. AFTER: (unchanged — "implications only work if the Reader gets them," and here the Reader does.)
**Exception:** Implication is legitimate and natural whenever the Reader restores it without stumbling.

### Whittle with the Zero Relative
**Rule:** In a restrictive clause where the relative pronoun is not the clause's subject, you may drop the pronoun; choose whichever version reads better.
**Detect:** noun + that/whom/which + own subject + verb in a comma-free clause ("the job that you wanted"). Not droppable when the pronoun is the clause's subject ("Rudy, who had always loved her") or the clause is nonrestrictive.
**Fix:**
1. Confirm the clause is restrictive (no commas).
2. Confirm the pronoun is immediately followed by the clause's own subject and verb.
3. Delete the pronoun; reread.
4. Keep the deletion if tighter and clear; restore if the sentence misparses or loses rhythm.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Grace got the promotion that you wanted. AFTER: Grace got the promotion you wanted.
- BEFORE: The song that she wrote that summer became a hit. AFTER: The song she wrote that summer became a hit. (Also kills the distracting double "that.")
**Exception:** Both forms are correct — restore "that" for rhythm or formality at will. Never drop a pronoun that is its clause's subject.
**Macro before micro:** if the sentence has a structural violation (empty existential, buried news, nominalized subject), fix that first — dropping a "that" from "There were many reasons that she should have stayed" treats a symptom and leaves the disease (see Collapse Empty Existentials, `structure.md`).

### That/Which by House Style
**Rule:** Under AP or Chicago style (news, most US business/publishing), introduce restrictive clauses with "that" and reserve "which" for comma-set-off nonrestrictive clauses.
**Detect:** "which" heading a comma-free (restrictive) clause in AP/Chicago-bound prose; conversely, "that" heading a comma-set-off clause.
**Fix:**
1. Run the restrictive/nonrestrictive removal test.
2. Restrictive + "which" → change to "that," no commas.
3. Nonrestrictive → "which" (who/whom for people), enclosed in commas.
4. If the text follows a style permitting restrictive "which," leave it.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Any apartment which I rent must allow dogs. AFTER: Any apartment that I rent must allow dogs.
- BEFORE: Any essay which she submits gets read twice. AFTER: (unchanged if not bound to AP/Chicago — restrictive "which" is standard English, not an error.)
**Exception:** Explicitly a style recommendation "you can choose to follow — or not." Not a grammar error.

### Verify Word Function Before Editing
**Rule:** Before applying any relative-clause or adverb rule, confirm the word's actual function; the same token does different jobs.
**Detect:** "that" after a cognition/communication verb (knew, said, learned, believed) heads a noun clause, not a relative clause; a "that" clause as sentence subject is a noun clause; "who" opening a question is interrogative; ", which" pointing at a whole preceding idea is a sentential relative (keep the comma, confirm the referent idea is clear). For adverbs: a word after a linking verb is an adjective ("I am well"); the same word after an action verb is an adverb ("I sing well"); whole phrases can be adverbials without being adverbs.
**Fix:**
1. Ask what the clause or word is doing: describing the noun before it (relative clause), serving as subject/object (noun clause), or answering when/where/manner (adverbial).
2. Apply relative-clause rules only to true relative clauses; leave noun clauses alone.
3. For sentential ", which," keep the comma and verify the referent idea is unambiguous.
4. For ambiguous word classes, check a dictionary before cutting.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Priya learned which life is not fair. AFTER: Priya learned that life is not fair. (Noun clause — a blind which/that style swap misfires here.)
- BEFORE: He quit on Friday which was fine by me. AFTER: He quit on Friday, which was fine by me. (Sentential relative: nonrestrictive, takes the comma.)
**Exception:** No need to memorize every job these words do — just notice a word's function before acting on it.

## Quick scan list
- **Nearest-Word Attachment** — PP not beside its logical target; a noun intervenes.
- **Un-Dangle Participles** — fronted -ing/-ed phrase + subject that isn't the doer.
- **Possessive Anchor** — fronted modifier + "X's Y" where the modifier describes X.
- **Ambiguous Pronouns** — third-person pronoun with 2+ matching candidate nouns.
- **Vague It/That/Which** — pronoun pointing at a verb, clause, or multi-idea sentence.
- **One List, One Form** — stem + item fails grammar or truth when distributed.
- **Restrictive Commas** — comma presence contradicts the clause-removal test.
- **Relative-Clause Overload** — 2+ that/which/who clauses, or "oh, by the way" backstory.
- **Modifier Pileup** — stacked modifiers where no reordering works; rewrite/split/colon.
- **Trailing-Modifier Scope** — "A, B, and C with X" where both scopes parse.
- **Manner-Adverb Deletion Test** — -ly/degree word on action verb; delete unless it adds meaning.
- **Show, Don't Tell Manner** — vivid verb + intensity adverb; swap for observable result.
- **Gerund/Participle Twins** — -ing + noun with two readings; insert "that" or recast.
- **Suspended Compounds** — bare attributive noun stranded before a shared plural tail.
- **Repeat Function Word** — single "by/to/in" over long or "as well as" items.
- **Ellipsis Check** — "X is, too." where restoration copies a long phrase.
- **Zero Relative** — droppable "that" before its clause's own subject + verb.
- **That/Which Style** — restrictive "which" in AP/Chicago prose.
- **Verify Function** — that/which/who or adverb doing a non-target job; don't misapply rules.
