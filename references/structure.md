# Structure: Clause Architecture, Emphasis, and Buried Action

Covers where the news of a sentence must sit (main clause), upside-down subordination, coordination vs. subordination, booby-trapped subordinators (as/while/since/if/than), nominalizations and buried verbs, passive voice (identification, conversion, deliberate use), and the strip-to-subject-and-verb surgery method.
Does NOT cover: sentence-length strategy (reader-first.md), modifier placement and relative-clause load (modifiers.md), punctuation (grammar-mechanics.md).

Governing test for every rule below: the Reader treats the main clause as the headline. Structure is judged by whether it serves the Reader, not by whether it obeys a rule — these are "safe havens," not laws.

Examples note: BEFORE/AFTER pairs model a writer revising their own text. When editing someone else's text, any AFTER that adds or narrows information beyond the BEFORE requires source support or an `[AUTHOR CHECK]` — the no-new-facts invariant in SKILL.md outranks every rule here.

### Main clause carries the main news
**Rule:** Put the most newsworthy, Reader-relevant information in the main clause; subordinate only setup or background. If a subordinate opener stacks multiple vivid actions before a weak main clause, split so each major action gets its own main clause.
**Detect:** Sentence-initial subordinator (after, although, as, because, before, if, once, since, though, unless, until, when, while; even though, as long as) introducing high-stakes action (violence, crime, major events), while the main clause holds a be-verb, stative verb, or weak realization (was tired, will remain, realized, felt). Chain variant: subordinator + 2+ coordinated action verbs, comma, then abstract subject (quest, mission, effort, situation) + weak verb (will remain, is, continues).
**Fix:**
1. Identify the main clause (the one that stands alone).
2. Rank every event in the sentence by what the Reader most wants to know.
3. If a subordinate clause outranks the main clause, swap: promote the big event to main-clause position; demote or delete the mundane one — or split into separate sentences, one main clause per major action.
4. Re-check that no conditional/temporal logic was lost or warped (dropping an "if" can falsify the facts).
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Before torching the warehouse, Ellen was a librarian. AFTER: After a decade as a librarian, Ellen torched the warehouse. (The arson — the news — moves to the main clause.)
- BEFORE: Until Marco can scale the wall, steal the crown, and bribe Chancellor Vex, his quest to free the prisoners will remain unfinished. AFTER: Marco must scale the wall. Then he must steal the crown and bribe Chancellor Vex. It's the only way to free the prisoners.
- BEFORE: If I'm going to leave you my beach house, you must maintain it. AFTER: I'm going to leave you my beach house on the condition that you maintain it. (The gift is promoted; the contingency survives.)
**Exception:** Keep the big event subordinated when a prior sentence already covered it, or when this Reader genuinely values the "mundane" clause more (a patient cares that the clinic resumed normal hours, not that a merger failed). Fiction may invert deliberately: Tim O'Brien downplays a death — "until he was shot in the head outside the village of Than Ke." Subordination must be a choice, never an accident.

### Main-clause X-ray and rebuild
**Rule:** Strip any problem sentence to its bare main subject + main verb; if that core states nothing new, rebuild around a concrete subject and an action verb.
**Detect:** The reduction is a tautology or blank-is-a-blank ("this depiction is a portrait," "a feature is a chance" — generic category noun = generic category noun). Abstract subject that needs an of-phrase to mean anything (examination of, rendering of, provision of, impact of). Main verb is "to be" or a copular substitute (emerges as, serves as, stands as, proves to be, represents). Three or more prepositional phrases piled up before the verb.
**Fix:**
1. Isolate main subject and main verb; read the bare clause: does it tell the Reader anything?
2. If not, name the concrete thing the sentence is actually about (the film, the bank, the teens) and make it the subject.
3. Find the action buried in a noun (depiction → depicts, confirmation → confirms, proposal → proposes) and make it the main verb.
4. Reattach leftover information as a second sentence, a coordinated verb, or by promoting a content noun to subject.
5. Cut adjectives and scaffolding that no longer earn their place.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: This tender and perceptive examination of the strain of deployment on spouses emerges as a moving portrait of military family life. AFTER: This tender film examines the strain of deployment on spouses left behind. (Bare clause was "examination emerges as portrait" — "a whole lotta nuthin'.")
- BEFORE: Another feature at the Science Camp is the chance for teens to build a robot. AFTER: Teens at the Science Camp can also build a robot.
- BEFORE: The report's central finding stands as a confirmation of what auditors suspected. AFTER: The report confirms what auditors suspected.
**Exception:** Several grammatical rebuilds may exist; pick by genre, tone, and Reader expectation (a film-festival blurb has its own register). An abstract noun may keep the subject slot when it truly is the topic and does real work. Plain "is" is fine when the complement genuinely informs.

### Unbury nominalizations
**Rule:** When an empty carrier verb hauls an abstract noun built from a verb or adjective, restore the root as the main verb (or as subject + be + adjective), with the real doer as subject.
**Detect:** Carrier verb (have, make, show, exhibit, give, achieve, perform, provide, experience, take place, occur) + noun with suffix -tion, -sion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -al, -ance, or a gerund, that has a verb/adjective root (utilization/utilize, refusal/refuse, happiness/happy). Also: nominalization as subject or inside a possessive/by/of phrase ("Lena's promotion by the board," "rejection of the manuscript") while the main verb is empty (was shocking, took place, was evident).
**Fix:**
1. Recover the root word (verb or adjective).
2. Find the actor: check the possessive, by-phrase, of-phrase, then surrounding context.
3. Verb root: write actor + root verb + object as the main clause. Adjective root: write subject + be + adjective; if the of-object is itself an action, make the adjective an adverb on that action ("stared intensely").
4. Express leftover evaluation (shocking, evident) as its own short sentence or clause.
5. Compare original vs. recast for wordiness, clarity, rhythm, emphasis; keep the winner.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The staff achieved utilization of the new software. AFTER: The staff utilized the new software.
- BEFORE: The rejection of the manuscript was shocking. AFTER: The editor rejected the manuscript. I was shocked.
- BEFORE: The intensity of her staring unsettled him. AFTER: She stared intensely, and it unsettled him. (Double nominalization unwound to actor + verb + adverb.)
- BEFORE: Lena's promotion by the board took place Friday. AFTER: On Friday the board promoted Lena.
**Exception:** Nominalizations are legitimate words — flag and compare, never auto-rewrite. "I appreciate Elena's encouragement" beats "Elena encourages me and I appreciate it." Necessary technical terms always stay.
**Presupposition caution:** unburying must not promote presuppositions to assertions. "The analysis of the impact of the closure on families" presupposes an impact but does not assert one — recast as "officials will study how the closure affects families" only if the source asserts the impact; otherwise keep the hedge in the body text ("will study whether/how the closure affects families"), not just in an [AUTHOR CHECK].

### Kill "the + gerund + of"
**Rule:** Rewrite every "the [verb]-ing of [noun]": first pare to bare gerund + direct object; then, usually better, recast with a real actor performing a finite verb.
**Detect:** Literal token sequence: "the" + -ing word with a verb root + "of" + noun phrase ("the walking of the dog," "the considering of the offer"). Second-stage trigger: sentence subject is a doer-less gerund phrase with a vague predicate (is part of, is good exercise, is crucial, will help).
**Fix:**
1. Delete "the" and "of"; the gerund takes the noun as direct object.
2. Then find the doer from context; if none, use "you," a generic subject, or the frame "It's [adjective] that [subject] [verb]."
3. Write doer + finite root verb + object as the main clause; move the old predicate to a coordinated clause or separate sentence.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The reciting of the pledge is part of the assembly. AFTER: Reciting the pledge is part of the assembly. (Stage 1: three words cut, action foregrounded.)
- BEFORE: Singing "Sweet Caroline" is part of the ceremony. AFTER: The graduates sing "Sweet Caroline" as part of the ceremony. (Stage 2: named actor, finite verb.)
- BEFORE: Remembering the deadline is crucial. AFTER: It's crucial that you remember the deadline.
**Exception:** Deliberate detachment or formality occasionally wants the action objectified and the doer downplayed; the bare-gerund stage alone may suffice. Genre note: the construction barely appears in prizewinning fiction — treat it as near-always wrong in narrative prose.

### Un-stuff "has + noun + participle"
**Rule:** When a sentence is built on have/has/had + noun + present participle, promote the participle to finite main verb with its noun as subject.
**Detect:** Exact frame: form of "have" + noun phrase + -ing verb ("has investors speculating," "had residents wondering"); the sentence's newest, most important information sits inside the participial phrase, not the main clause.
**Fix:**
1. Make the noun the subject and the participle the finite main verb ("investors are speculating").
2. Recast the old subject as a cause: a since/because clause, a preceding sentence followed by "So," or "Why? Because...".
3. While rejiggering, unbury other actions from nouns (decision → decided, plan → plans).
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The council's plan to widen the boulevard has merchants worrying that foot traffic may be vanishing. AFTER: Since the council announced plans to widen the boulevard, merchants are worrying foot traffic may vanish.
- BEFORE: The league's decision to shorten the season has coaches predicting records may be falling. AFTER: The league decided to shorten the season. So coaches are predicting records may fall.
**Exception:** The fix rejiggers the whole sentence — confirm the new emphasis and rhythm serve the passage before committing.

### Unstick clause-as-noun subjects
**Rule:** Break "That + clause is the reason that / is why / is what + clause" into two plain sentences.
**Detect:** Sentence opens with "That ..." or "The fact that ..." serving as grammatical subject, followed by "is the reason (that)," "is why," or "is what" plus a second clause.
**Fix:**
1. Write the subject clause as a standalone sentence.
2. Write the trailing clause as a second sentence.
3. Join with "So" or "Because," or leave the causal link implicit.
4. Delete "is the reason that" entirely.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: That she trains so hard is the reason that she's making the team. AFTER: She trains hard. So she's making the team.
- BEFORE: The fact that sales dropped is what prompted the layoffs. AFTER: Sales dropped. So the company cut jobs.
**Exception:** A skilled writer may keep the structure deliberately for rhythm or emphasis.

### Collapse empty existentials and clefts
**Rule:** When "there is/are" or "it is/was ... that/who" adds no deliberate emphasis, promote the real subject and verb; this macro-fix takes precedence over micro-fixes (zero relative, fat trimming) on the same sentence.
**Detect:** "There is/are/was/were + noun + who/that/which..." where the sentence's real content sits in the relative clause; "It is/was X that/who..." clefts without an emphasis purpose; "There are many X who have Y-ed" shapes. Do not fire on existentials that genuinely introduce new information for emphasis (see `grammar-mechanics.md` §1 variations).
**Fix:**
1. Find the real subject (the noun after there-is/it-is) and the real action (in the relative clause).
2. Rewrite as subject + verb: "There are many residents who have complained" → "Many residents have complained."
3. If no verb hides in a relative clause, consider whether the existence claim itself is the point; if it is, keep the existential.
4. Do not "fix" the sentence by deleting only the relative pronoun — that treats the symptom and leaves the structure.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: There were many reasons that she should have stayed. AFTER: She had many reasons to stay.
- BEFORE: It was the intern who caught the error. AFTER: The intern caught the error. (Unless contrast with someone else is the point — then the cleft earns its keep.)
- BEFORE: There are aliens in the building. AFTER: (unchanged — the existence of aliens IS the news; the existential is doing deliberate work.)
**Exception:** Existentials and clefts are legitimate emphasis devices when spotlighting new information or contrast is the goal. The target is the reflexive, emphasis-free habit.

### True-passive test (don't misdiagnose)
**Rule:** Label a sentence passive only when a be-form + past participle of a transitive verb puts the action's receiver in the subject slot — never merely because it contains "to be" with an -ing word or an adjective.
**Detect:** Scan be-forms (is/was/were/been/being) + the next word. Next word ends in -ing → progressive, active. Next word is an adjective with no verb sense (coy, evasive) → active. Next word is a past participle (-ed/-en; woken, driven, drunk, spoken, thought) AND appending "by [someone]" reads sensibly AND the subject undergoes the action → true passive.
**Fix:**
1. Locate each be-form and classify the following word.
2. -ing or adjective: mark active; fix any wordiness, not voice.
3. Past participle: apply the "by [someone]" test.
4. Only confirmed passives proceed to the three passive rules below.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE (misflagged): Nora was strolling down the avenue. AFTER: unchanged — progressive, active; Nora is the doer. No voice fix.
- BEFORE (misflagged): Marcus was being coy. AFTER: unchanged — "coy" is an adjective; no one is "coying" Marcus.
- BEFORE: Marcus was being watched. AFTER: Someone was watching Marcus. (Same shape as the coy sentence, but genuinely passive and convertible.)
**Exception:** Ugly strings of be + -ing ("had been considering doing some planning") are horrible but active — fix the flab, not the voice.

### Activate action-squelching passives
**Rule:** Convert a passive to active — actor as subject, action verb as main verb — whenever the passive dilutes dramatic or interesting action; supply a missing actor honestly.
**Detect:** Be-form + past participle of a vivid transitive verb (shot, stabbed, chased, rammed, smashed) with the actor demoted to a by-phrase or dropped; especially narrative prose where events should feel immediate.
**Fix:**
1. Confirm a true passive (rule above), then draft the active counterpart.
2. Move the by-phrase actor to the subject slot; conjugate the participle as main verb in the original tense; former subject becomes object; delete the auxiliary and "by."
3. No by-phrase: pull the actor from context; else use "someone" or a plausible role noun ("a burglar"). Never assert a specific actor you don't know for a fact.
4. Adopt the active version only if the actor and action carry the interest; if not, keep the passive.
5. Terminal state: when the source names no actor and none is honestly inferable, keeping the passive plus an `[AUTHOR CHECK: who did this?]` is a complete, compliant fix — do not force a recast that invents a doer.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Trevor was stabbed by Elaine. AFTER: Elaine stabbed Trevor. (The passive form is anemic by comparison.)
- BEFORE: The jewelry was stolen. AFTER: A burglar stole the jewelry. (Generic role noun supplies an honest subject; leaving it passive is also fine when the doer is unknown.)
- BEFORE: Lena was being followed. AFTER: Someone was following Lena.
**Exception:** "The tourist was eaten by the shark" stays passive — the tourist's fate, not the shark, is the story. The receiver-as-topic case belongs to the next rule.

### Keep the passive to downplay the doer
**Rule:** Choose the passive deliberately when the passage is about the receiver of the action, or when the natural active subject is a vague crowd that would hijack the topic.
**Detect:** Keep-signals: the active subject would be people/voters/critics/experts/moviegoers or an actor the passage isn't about; reputation formulas (is considered, is widely regarded as, is known as); activating would demote the passage's real topic from the subject slot.
**Fix:**
1. Identify the noun the passage is about.
2. If that noun receives the action, cast it as subject via the passive.
3. Drop the by-phrase when the actor is obvious, unknown, or beside the point.
4. Verify the omission serves the Reader's focus rather than hiding a claim you were too lazy to substantiate.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The shareholders re-elected the chairwoman. AFTER: The chairwoman was re-elected. (Keeps the sentence about her, not the shareholders.)
- BEFORE: Critics regard Washington as one of the finest actors of his generation. AFTER: Washington is widely regarded as one of the finest actors of his generation. (The active flip invites "Which critics?")
**Exception:** "Is considered" can be weasel cover for unsubstantiated assertions; omit the anointer only when the sidestep genuinely serves the Reader.

### Restructure passive pileups
**Rule:** When two or more passives chain in a sentence or adjacent clauses, rewrite the whole passage — actors supplied, sentences split — instead of flipping each clause in place.
**Detect:** Two or more be + past-participle constructions in one sentence or adjacent clauses; stacked auxiliaries ("had been X-ed and had been Y-ed ... was Z-ed by"); long subordinate openers built entirely of passives.
**Fix:**
1. List every action and its actual doer (infer actors from context).
2. Split the passage at natural event boundaries.
3. Recast each event with its doer as subject and an active verb.
4. Cut redundant auxiliaries and prepositional filler ("on the phone by").
5. Reread for sequence and flow.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: After she had been driven to Denver and had been booked into her hotel, she was contacted by phone by her manager. AFTER: The firm drove her to Denver. After she checked in, her manager called.
- BEFORE: Once the samples had been collected and had been labeled by the interns, the results were reviewed by Dr. Kim. AFTER: The interns collected and labeled the samples. Then Dr. Kim reviewed the results.
**Exception:** One well-chosen passive may survive the rewrite if its receiver is the topic; the target is the pileup, not every passive.

### Coordinate equals, subordinate to encode relationships
**Rule:** Join clauses of equal news value with a coordinator (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so — FANBOYS) or split into sentences; use a subordinator only to encode a real time/cause/concession relationship, attached to the background clause.
**Detect:** Wrong subordination: although/while/since/as joining two clauses of comparable value where neither is setup — test: each stands alone and neither is background to the other. Wrong coordination: "clause and clause" hiding an implied causal or temporal link; or a subordinator asserting a relationship the facts don't support.
**Fix:**
1. Ask: does one clause depend on the other in time, cause, or contrast?
2. No → coordinator ("but" for contrast) or two sentences; verify the clauses could swap order without loss.
3. Yes → pick the subordinator that names that exact relationship (after, because, although, when, until) and attach it to the background clause.
4. Generate rearrangements — fronted vs. trailing subordinate clause, different subordinator, split — and keep the one that says exactly what you mean.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: While Dana plays guitar, she also sings. AFTER: Dana plays guitar and she also sings. (Two equal facts; "while" arbitrarily demoted one.)
- BEFORE: Though I like oranges, I devour tangerines. AFTER: I like oranges but I devour tangerines.
- BEFORE: The storm hit and the flight was canceled. AFTER: Because the storm hit, the flight was canceled. (Subordination makes the causal link explicit.)
**Exception:** No arrangement is inherently correct — coordination, any of several subordinators, or two separate sentences are all valid; intended meaning decides. "Subordination is not a bad thing. It's a tool."

### "As" means simultaneous
**Rule:** Use "as" only to join genuinely simultaneous actions; never chain sequential events with it, and never let the as-clause swallow the main action.
**Detect:** Clause + "as" + clause(s) where the events form a sequence or cause-effect (a blow, then a fall; an impact, then flames). Fiction action scenes where the main clause is a reaction shot (a look came over his face, her heart sank) and the as-clause holds the real event.
**Fix:**
1. Ask: do the actions truly happen at the same instant?
2. Sequential → split into sentences or use "then"/"after" to show order.
3. Promote the most dramatic action to a main clause.
4. Keep "as" only for literal overlap.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: A bewildered look came over Petra's face as the spear pierced her shield and she fell. AFTER: The spear pierced Petra's shield. She fell from the rampart, bewildered.
- BEFORE: The engine burst into flames as the jet slammed onto the runway. AFTER: The jet slammed onto the runway and skidded to a stop. Then the engine burst into flames.
**Exception:** True simultaneity is the correct use: "She hummed as she washed the dishes" — leave it.

### Definition traps: while, since, if, than
**Rule:** Audit these four subordinators for misreadings; recast whenever the Reader could parse the wrong meaning first.
**Detect:**
- *while* (concessive): substitute "during the time that" — if nonsense, the use is concessive; flag if the opener could momentarily read as temporal ("While swimming laps...").
- *since* (causal): sentence-initial "Since" + completed event ("Since you've graduated...") followed by a request or conclusion — the time-span reading is initially plausible.
- *if* (false contingency): "If you [enjoy/love/are]..." + a main clause stating a standing fact that holds regardless. Test: negate the if-clause; is the main clause still true?
- *than* (ambiguous elision): comparative + "than" + bare noun that could be subject or object of the elided verb. Test: expand both readings; are both plausible?
**Fix:**
1. while → replace with "although" when a temporal misread is possible.
2. since → replace with "because" when a time-span misread is possible; genuinely temporal "since" stays.
3. if → recast as "for those who X," or make the main clause genuinely contingent ("you'll be happy that...").
4. than → spell out the intended full clause ("more than you like X" / "more than X likes it").
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: While swimming laps is good exercise, rowing is better. AFTER: Although swimming laps is good exercise, rowing is better.
- BEFORE: Since you've worked at NASA, can you tell me what the engineers are like? AFTER: Because you've worked at NASA, can you tell me what the engineers are like?
- BEFORE: If you love jazz, the lounge features live trios every Friday. AFTER: For those who love jazz, the lounge features live trios every Friday. (The bookings don't depend on the Reader's taste.)
- BEFORE: Do you like Radiohead more than Björk? AFTER: Do you like Radiohead more than you like Björk?
**Exception:** Dictionaries allow concessive "while" and causal "since" — recast only when misreading is plausible. Idiomatic false ifs pass ("If you need me, I'll be at my desk"). Than-elision is fine when only one reading works ("Nadia runs faster than Stefan").

### Parse units before moving them
**Rule:** Before any restructuring, classify each unit (clause vs. phrase), locate the main clause, and move phrases only as whole blocks.
**Detect:** Toolkit, applied mechanically:
- Clause = subject paired with a tensed (finite) verb; can stand alone. Phrase = one or more words acting as a single part of speech; cannot.
- Find clauses: list every action verb, pair each with its doer; any pair led by a subordinator is dependent; at least one unsubordinated pair — the main clause — must exist, or the sentence is a fragment.
- A phrase's type is its headword's job in this sentence, not the word's dictionary label ("veteran" can be noun or adjective).
- Phrases nest like dolls: move the outermost block intact ("happily married" travels together; stranding "happily" changes what it modifies).
- Preposition + its object = one inseparable team. Object test: swap the noun for her/him/it/them — if grammatical, it's an object, and it closes the verb or prepositional phrase.
**Fix:**
1. Bracket every movable phrase and clause.
2. Generate variants: swap clause order, front or postpone the subordinate clause, change the subordinator, split into two sentences.
3. Pick the arrangement that matches the intended meaning and emphasis exactly — sentences are modular, movable, interlocking pieces.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Although the witness testified. The jury doubted her. AFTER: Although the witness testified, the jury doubted her. (The subordinated pair joins the main clause; alone it was a fragment.)
- BEFORE: Devon hired a talented highly engineer. AFTER: Devon hired a highly talented engineer. ("Highly" hinges on "talented"; the nested block moves whole.)
**Exception:** Imperatives ("Run like the wind!") and nonfinite clauses ("Joe wanted to cry," "Nadia doesn't like crying") are complete — do not add subjects or conjugate them; the "fix" adds words and kills directness. Fragments in dialogue and casual registers are fine when chosen deliberately.

- **Main clause carries the main news** — sentence-initial subordinator + vivid action, then a weak/stative main clause.

- **Unbury nominalizations** — carrier verb (have/make/show/achieve) + -tion/-ment/-ness/-ity noun; nominalized subject + empty verb (took place, was evident).
- **Kill "the + gerund + of"** — literal "the [verb]-ing of"; doer-less gerund subject + vague predicate.
- **Un-stuff "has + noun + participle"** — have/has/had + noun + -ing verb carrying the real news.
- **Unstick clause-as-noun subjects** — "That.../The fact that..." + "is the reason that / is why / is what."
- **Collapse empty existentials and clefts** — "There is/are X who/that..." or purposeless "It was X that..." → promote the real subject and verb.
- **True-passive test** — be-form + next word: -ing or adjective = active; transitive past participle + "by [someone]" fits = passive.
- **Activate action-squelching passives** — be + vivid transitive participle, actor in by-phrase or missing, in narrative prose.
- **Keep the passive to downplay the doer** — receiver is the topic; active subject would be a vague crowd; is considered / is regarded as.
- **Restructure passive pileups** — 2+ be+participle constructions chained; stacked "had been X-ed" auxiliaries.
- **Coordinate equals, subordinate to encode** — subordinator joining equal-value clauses; "and" hiding a causal/temporal link.
