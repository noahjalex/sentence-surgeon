# Grammar & Mechanics Reference (Casagrande)

Lookup reference for an editor mid-rewrite: sentence structures and their variations, phrase/clause taxonomy, verb machinery (types, participles, aspect, mood), pronoun case, tense selection, punctuation, and the iconic credibility-killing errors.
This file is reference material only. Style judgments (length, rhythm, passive voice, subordination strategy, word choice) live in sibling files.

---

## 1. Five basic sentence structures

| # | Structure | Pattern | Example |
|---|-----------|---------|---------|
| 1 | Intransitive | S + V | The ferry departs. |
| 2 | Transitive | S + V + direct object | I see Betty. |
| 3 | Copular | S + linking V + subject complement | Rex smells terrible. |
| 4 | Ditransitive | S + V + indirect object + direct object | Jake sends Mary love letters. |
| 5 | Complex-transitive | S + V + object + object complement | Spinach makes Pete strong. |

- Every structure may add **adverbials** (adverbs, prepositional phrases, time noun phrases, adverbial clauses). Adverbials are structurally optional: delete each in turn — the residue must still be grammatical. Diagnostic only; an adverbial may carry crucial information ("The ferry departs **at dawn**").
- **Indirect-object swap** (structure 2 ↔ 4): with give/send/make/bake/tell + DO + to/for + recipient, move recipient before DO and drop the preposition, or reverse. Both orders grammatical; choose by rhythm. "Carla baked cookies for her son" ↔ "Carla baked her son cookies."

### Variations (emphasis devices — deliberate, not defaults)

| Device | Shape | Effect | Example |
|--------|-------|--------|---------|
| Fragment | Standalone phrase, no S+finite V | Punch | "That's what he craved. Freedom." |
| Cleft | It + is/was + X + who/that + rest | Spotlights X | "It was Marta who won the case." |
| Existential | There + is/are + notional subject | Spotlights new info | "There are rats in the cellar." |
| Anticipatory *it* | It + V + C + that-clause | Unburies clause-as-subject | "It is wonderful news that you passed the bar." |
| Left dislocation | NP, + pronoun copy | Colloquial topic focus | "Cars, they're not what they used to be." |
| Right dislocation | Clause, + trailing NP | Afterthought focus | "They have a lot of money, Carol and Bill." |
| Fronting | Adverbial/phrase moved to front | Resets emphasis | "After Diego bowed, Mara clapped." |
| Intonation question | Statement + ? (no inversion) | Spoken register | "You'll be there on time?" |

Genre calibration: fragments are "completely acceptable in fiction and nonfiction — especially in informal contexts"; formal contexts may bar them. Clefts/existentials add words and shift the grammatical subject — use only when emphasis is the goal; an emphasis-free "There are many X who..." is a violation, not a device (Collapse Empty Existentials and Clefts, `structure.md`).

### Operator mechanics (negation & questions)

- **Operator** = first word of the verb phrase (be/have/modal auxiliary).
- Negation: insert *not* immediately after the operator, before any participle. "Denise has **not** trained hard."
- Question: invert subject and operator. "Has Denise trained hard?"
- No auxiliary present → insert dummy operator **do** (conjugated), reduce main verb to base form. "Your sons play chess" → "**Do** your sons play chess?"
- Do not "correct" intonation questions in dialogue/informal prose.

## 2. Phrase taxonomy

| Type | Headword | Job | Example |
|------|----------|-----|---------|
| Noun phrase | noun/pronoun | names a thing | "the champion of the tango circuit" |
| Verb phrase | verb | conveys action/state | "has danced" |
| Adjective phrase | adjective | modifies noun | "highly talented" |
| Adverb phrase | adverb | modifies verb/adj | "very quickly" |
| Prepositional phrase | preposition + object | modifier team | "with a rusty chain" |

Working rules:
- **Headword decides type by function in this sentence**, not dictionary label ("veteran" = noun in one sentence, adjective in another). One-word phrases count.
- **Nesting dolls:** phrases contain phrases ("with a [happily married] septuagenarian woman"). Move/cut the whole nested block — stranding an inner word changes what modifies what.
- **Preposition + object travel as a team**; park the whole phrase next to the word it modifies, or get "bike for sale by owner with a rusty chain."
- **Pronoun-swap object test:** replace the NP with her/him/it/them. Works → it is an object (of a transitive verb or preposition); marks where the phrase ends. Fails → reanalyze (likely subject).

## 3. Clause taxonomy

| Term | Definition | Standalone? |
|------|------------|-------------|
| Clause | Subject paired with conjugated (finite) verb | Main clause: yes |
| Phrase | 1+ words acting as one part of speech | Never |
| Main clause | S+V pair with no subordinator | Yes |
| Subordinate clause | S+V pair led by subordinator (because, if, although, when, until, after...) | No — fragment alone |
| Relative clause | who/which/that clause modifying the preceding noun | No |
| Finite clause | Conjugated verb expressing time | — |
| Nonfinite clause | Infinitive ("to cry") or -ing form ("crying"); no time marking | No, but legitimate as complement |
| Imperative | Command; implied subject "you" | Yes — "Stop!" is complete |

Parsing procedure: list every action verb → pair each with its doer → flag subordinator-led pairs as dependent → confirm at least one unsubordinated pair (the main clause). All pairs subordinated = fragment: delete a subordinator or attach to a main clause.

Joining clauses:
- **Coordinators** (and, but, or; also for, nor, so, yet) link grammatical equals; clauses should be swappable in order.
- **Subordinators** encode a specific time/cause/concession relationship coordination cannot ("Because the storm hit, the flight was canceled").
- Two separate sentences = third valid option for blunt equal weight. No arrangement is inherently correct; choose by exact intended meaning. Phrases and clauses are modular — generate variants by moving them, then pick.
- Do not over-correct imperatives ("Run!") or nonfinite complements ("Joe wanted to cry") into padded finite clauses.

## 4. Verb machinery

| Verb type | Takes | Test | Complement rule |
|-----------|-------|------|-----------------|
| Transitive | direct object | pronoun-swap works after it | — |
| Intransitive | no object | swap fails | manner adverbs fine |
| Copular (be, seem, appear, act, feel, smell, taste, sound, look, become) | subject complement | substitute "is" — meaning holds | **adjective, never -ly adverb**: "I feel bad," "Rex smells terrible" |

- Copular trap: many of these verbs have noncopular senses that take adverbs/objects. "Omar acts badly" = unskilled actor (keep adverb); "Fido smells meat" = transitive. Decide meaning first.
- **Verbals** (verb forms doing another job): gerund (-ing as noun), participle (-ing/-ed/-en as modifier), infinitive (to + base as noun/adj/adv).
- **Participles:** present = -ing; past = -ed/-en (irregulars listed in bold in dictionary entries — that is where to check; no forms listed = regular verb).
- **Aspect:** perfect = have + past participle (completed); progressive = be + present participle (ongoing).
- **Mood:** indicative (statements/questions), imperative (commands, implied "you"), subjunctive (see below).
- **Modal auxiliaries:** can, may, might, could, must, should, will, shall, ought to, would — combine with *have/be/do*, never with the preposition *of* (why "could of" is always wrong).

### Subjunctive (surviving uses only)
| Trigger | Form | Example |
|---------|------|---------|
| Contrary-to-fact / wish with *to be*, past | **were** (all persons) | "If I were rich..." |
| That-clause after demand, insist, suggest, propose, require, "it is essential that" | base form | "demands that he **resign**"; "insisted that the report **be** filed" |

Do not resurrect dead subjunctive constructions; outside these two patterns, subjunctive = indicative in form.

## 5. Pronoun case

| Subject form | Object form |
|--------------|-------------|
| I, he, she, we, they, who | me, him, her, us, them, whom |

- **Object of any preposition takes object form** — including every member of a compound: "between you and **me**," "to **him** or **me**." Test compounds by dropping the other member: "Talk to I" fails.
- Hypercorrection ("between you and I") is flatly ungrammatical; no exceptions given.

## 5a. Subject–verb agreement

The verb agrees with its subject's **head noun**, not with whatever noun sits closest to the verb.

| Trap | Wrong | Right | Test |
|------|-------|-------|------|
| Attraction across a PP | "The box of parts were shipped" | "The box of parts **was** shipped" | Strip prepositional phrases; agree with the head noun (box) |
| Expletive there | "There's three problems" | "There **are** three problems" | The notional subject follows the verb — agree with it |
| Either/or, neither/nor | "Neither the manager nor the clerks was told" | "Neither the manager nor the clerks **were** told" | Agree with the nearer subject |
| Collective nouns | — | "The committee **is** meeting" (unit) / "The committee **are** divided" (members; rare in US English — usually recast: "committee members are divided") | Decide unit vs. members; US default is singular |
| One of the X who | "one of the editors who insists" (when all insist) | "one of the editors who **insist**" | The who-clause modifies the plural X; agree plural unless "the only one" |
| Compound subject with and | "The CEO and the CFO was there" | "The CEO and the CFO **were** there" | And-joined subjects are plural (unless a single unit: "mac and cheese is") |

Detection routine: for every finite verb, find the subject's head noun (ignore intervening of/with/along-with phrases), then check number.

## 7. Tense selection

Families: **progressive** = be + -ing (ongoing); **perfect** = have + past participle (completed by the time discussed). "Often the simplest verb tense is the best verb tense."

### Prefer the simplest tense; complex tenses need an "another"
**Rule:** Collapse stacked-auxiliary tenses to the simplest tense that keeps the meaning; use perfect/perfect-progressive forms only to relate one action's timing to another event, and keep the sentence's main event in a simple tense.
**Detect:** Two or more auxiliaries before the main verb (had been -ing, will have been -ing); progressive on stative verbs ("isn't wanting"); a perfect form with no when-clause, after-phrase, or adjacent simple-tense event; the climactic event itself in a perfect tense while background sits in simple past.
**Fix:**
1. Find verb phrases with 2+ auxiliaries or progressive statives.
2. Search the sentence and neighbors for the "another" event the timing is relative to.
3. None → rewrite in simple past/present matching the passage's timeframe.
4. One exists → keep the complex tense on the background action; put the main event in a simple tense (swap if inverted).
5. Reread: no sequence/continuity meaning lost.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The grandfather hadn't been wanting to go to Arizona. AFTER: The grandfather didn't want to go to Arizona. (No second event; collapse.)
- BEFORE: Priya had been working at the archive for a decade. [no second event ever comes] AFTER: Priya worked at the archive for a decade.
- BEFORE: After Boeing and SpaceX rejected him, Lucky had gotten a job at a diner. AFTER: After having been rejected by Boeing and SpaceX, Lucky got a job at a diner. (Main event takes simple past.)
- BEFORE: Jane made breakfast when she heard the crash. AFTER: Jane had been making breakfast when she heard the crash. (Interrupted continuing action genuinely needs the complex form.)
**Exception:** "There are times when these convoluted tenses capture your meaning and mood exactly." Keep them for relative timing, interruption, or mood; fiction may load up on them if the writer can pull it off.

### Default to simple past for completed events
**Rule:** Report completed events in simple past — the standard of straight news and most professional fiction; reserve sustained present-tense narration for short/experimental pieces, and hand off any present-tense overture to past tense with a time adverbial.
**Detect:** Narrative body verbs in simple present or present progressive for finished events; past time markers with present verbs ("approves the fare increase last week"); present-tense narration running many paragraphs of a long piece; a present-tense opening with no anchoring adverbial ("Last night," "In 1998") marking the shift.
**Fix:**
1. Ask: was the event completed before writing time? Yes → simple past.
2. Still unfolding at reading time (ongoing preparations, negotiations) → keep present progressive.
3. Deliberate sustained present-tense device → leave, but verify consistency throughout.
4. Present-tense overview opener → keep timeless material present, insert a time adverbial at the story's start, convert all verbs after the anchor to simple past.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Emboldened rebel fighters impose control over the valley. AFTER: Emboldened rebel fighters imposed control over the valley.
- BEFORE: Different things frighten us. I drive home through fog and think about my father. AFTER: Different things frighten us. Last night I drove home through fog and thought about my father. (Adverbial anchors the shift.)
- BEFORE: The ministry prepared to admit the refugees. AFTER: The ministry is preparing to admit the refugees. (Action continues at reading time — progressive correct.)
**Exception:** Present-tense narration of past events is a valid gamble for immediacy (the feature-lead device; a Kincaid-length experiment). Only 5 of 35 anthology stories use it; rarer still in novels. "Simple past tense is the standard form. It's a safe choice."

### Anchor once, then simplify
**Rule:** Let one complex-tense sentence establish the timeframe, then shift subsequent same-timeframe sentences to simple past.
**Detect:** Two+ consecutive sentences in had + participle / had been + -ing about one timeframe; chains like "had woken... had heard... had had."
**Fix:**
1. Keep the first complex-tense sentence as the time anchor.
2. Convert every later verb in that timeframe to simple past.
3. Repair stranded auxiliaries ("had had" → "had"; "had she finally gotten" → "did she finally get").
4. Confirm the anchor alone still places the passage in time.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Marta had slept for hours. She had dreamed about ships. She had woken when she had heard noises. AFTER: Marta had slept for hours. She dreamed about ships. She woke when she heard noises.
- BEFORE: The snow had been falling for days. It had buried Devon's car. AFTER: The snow had been falling for days. It buried Devon's car.
**Exception:** Staying in past perfect longer is grammatically fine — this is a readability move ("usually"), and some passages need more than one anchoring sentence.

### No accidental tense shifts (remember the when)
**Rule:** Keep every verb describing one time period in one tense; never drift between past and present within a timeframe.
**Detect:** Coordinated verbs or relative clauses mixing tenses about one timeframe: "was... who is," "seared... then plates"; past main clause with present list items and no still-true-fact or anchored-device justification.
**Fix:**
1. Identify the passage's time period (its "when").
2. List every verb referring to it.
3. Conform any stray verb to the passage's tense.
4. Exempt only still-true facts (next rule) and deliberate, anchored device shifts.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Roberto was an excellent swimmer who is kind. AFTER: Roberto was an excellent swimmer who was kind.
- BEFORE: The chef seared the fish, then plates it with lemon. AFTER: The chef seared the fish, then plated it with lemon.
**Exception:** None — the book calls this a plain error, not a style choice.

### Still-true facts stay present
**Rule:** Inside past narration, put a subordinate-clause fact in present tense when it remains true and its ongoing truth is what matters to the Reader. Scope: timeless or general truths only — never reported commitments, plans, or promises ("announced that it would offer..."), whose current status is unknowable from the text; leave their modality alone or flag `[AUTHOR CHECK]`.
**Detect:** Past reporting verb (discovered, explained, said, learned) + that-clause stating a general/scientific/ongoing truth rendered in past ("discovered that the earth revolved").
**Fix:**
1. Find that-clauses under past main verbs.
2. Ask: still true now, and is that what's notable?
3. Yes to both → embedded verb to simple present (main verb stays past).
4. Time-bound fact, or pastness is the point → keep past.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Kepler was thrilled when he discovered that the planets moved in ellipses. AFTER: Kepler was thrilled when he discovered that the planets move in ellipses.
- BEFORE: The witness said the door was unlocked that night. AFTER: The witness said the door was unlocked that night. (Time-bound — keep past.)
**Exception:** Both tenses are grammatical; the choice hinges on what is more notable to the Reader.

## 8. Punctuation reference

Governing principle: prevent Reader confusion, not rule-worship. Know which choices are absolute and which are style splits.

### Comma

| Use | Rule | Example |
|-----|------|---------|
| Series (3+) | Pick one serial-comma policy — AP: omit before final conjunction; Chicago/academic: include — and apply it identically document-wide. Mixing is the only error. | "kale, carrots and beets" (AP) / "kale, carrots, and beets" (Chicago) |
| Stacked modifiers | **And-test + swap-test:** comma only if "and" could replace it AND order can reverse. Either fails → no comma (cumulative). | "a rude, ugly, dishonest clown" vs "a bright red Hawaiian dress" |
| Introductory matter | Comma after intro phrase/clause; the longer the intro, the more necessary (~5+ words: flag). Very short intros may drop it. | "On the first Friday of any month when she is on call, Marta arrives early." / "On Mondays Zeke shows up early." |
| Compound sentence | Comma before coordinator joining two independent clauses; droppable when both clauses are short, clear, and joined by "and." | "We ate the salad, but the bread went untouched." / "I like apples and I like pears." |
| Nonrestrictive clause | Pair of commas if deleting the clause still points to the same referent; **no commas if it restricts** — wrong commas change the claim. | "Employees who missed the deadline were penalized" (only those) vs "Employees, who missed the deadline, were penalized" (all did) |
| Appositive | Commas when the rename restricts nothing ("Rosa's brother, Daniel," — only brother); none when it identifies which one. Titles can go either way by intent: "Her book, The Rogue," vs "Her book The Rogue." | — |
| Interjections | Set off (of course, indeed, yes); droppable when crowding another comma. | "Bombo could tell you, but of course then..." |
| Direct address | "Hello, Jacob" takes the comma; "Dear Jacob" does not (dear = adjective). | — |
| Pairs must close | ", Inc.", ", Jr.", and mid-sentence full dates open a pair — close it. | "May 2, 1990, and left"; "XYZ, Inc., and Apple" |
| too/either/also | Surrounding commas fading; optional. | — |

### Apostrophe

| Form | Rule | Example |
|------|------|---------|
| Singular possessive | add 's | the cat's tail |
| Plural in -s | apostrophe only | the dogs' tails |
| Plural not in -s | add 's | the children's mother |
| Singular ending in -s | Style split: Chicago **Charles's**, AP **Charles'** — follow the guide or pick one method, never mix | James's bike (Chicago) |
| Contraction | marks omitted letters/numbers | it's = it is/has |
| **Plurals** | **NEVER** — no "Laptop's for sale," no "the Garcia's." Pluralize names first (Garcia → Garcias, Jones → Joneses), then possessive if needed. | Season's greetings from the Garcias |
| Readability exception | Pluralize with apostrophe only to avoid misreading: A's (vs "As"); some publications extend it (1980's — NYT; 1980s — LAT) | got Cs, Bs, and a few A's |

Typography: the apostrophe curves like a closing single quote — never an opening one.

### Quotation marks (standard American)

| Mark | Placement |
|------|-----------|
| Period, comma | ALWAYS inside the closing quote |
| Colon, semicolon | ALWAYS outside |
| ?, ! | Logic-dependent: inside if the quoted matter asks/exclaims; outside if the framing sentence does |
| Quote within quote | single quotes |
| Capitalization | quoted complete sentence → capital; quoted fragment completed by unquoted words → lowercase |

- "It's over." not "It's over". | his "essentials": coffee | called me "champ"? | was "Who loves ya, baby?"
- Words-as-words, irony, and composition titles take quotes or italics per the governing style guide.

### Run-ons and comma splices
- **Run-on (fused sentence):** two independent clauses with no joining punctuation ("The report was late nobody noticed"). **Comma splice:** two independent clauses joined by only a comma ("The report was late, nobody noticed").
- Fix by one of: (1) period + new sentence; (2) comma + coordinating conjunction ("was late, but nobody"); (3) subordinate one clause ("Although the report was late, nobody noticed"); (4) semicolon only if the clauses are tightly linked and a period would be worse (see semicolon discipline, `concision.md`).
- Detect: scan every comma — if a subject + finite verb stands on each side and no conjunction follows the comma, it's a splice. Exception: very short parallel clauses in fiction/informal registers can splice deliberately ("I came, I saw, I conquered").

### One terminal mark only
Never double terminal punctuation: abbreviation period or quoted ?/! also ends the sentence. "He moved to Austin, Tex." — not "Tex..". A midsentence quoted question mark with the sentence continuing is legitimate: When he asked, "How are you?" it was as though he cared.

### Hyphen
- Hyphenate multiword modifiers **before** a noun when the bare version could be misread — "the main goal of the hyphen is to prevent confusion" ("a man-eating shark" vs "a man eating shark").
- **Never hyphenate after an -ly adverb:** "a happily married couple."
- Prefixes/suffixes/set compounds: look up (nondairy, midsentence, companywide typical; verb "water-ski" vs noun "water ski"). Hyphenation is "highly subjective" — no two editors match; the test is Reader confusion.

### Dashes, colon, semicolon, parentheses

| Mark | Use |
|------|-----|
| Em dash (—) | Abrupt changes, parenthetical asides, interruptions, emphasis; can replace comma pairs for emphasis |
| En dash (–) | Ranges ("1998–2002," inclusive); multiword compounds a hyphen can't handle ("World War I–era planes"); absent from most newspaper styles |
| Colon | Introduces lists, emphasis, multi-sentence quotations, formal salutations. Capital after colon: AP yes for complete sentences; Chicago only for 2+ sentences. Always outside closing quotes. |
| Semicolon | Joins closely related independent clauses; separates series items that contain commas. Always outside closing quotes. |
| Parentheses | De-emphasize (less important than the main passage; comma pairs = neutral, em dashes = emphasize). Terminal period inside only when the parenthetical stands alone as a full sentence. |

## 9. The iconic credibility-killing errors

### Kill the iconic errors first
**Rule:** Scan for and correct the specific confusions that brand a writer an amateur before polishing anything else; these cost more credibility than any number of ordinary typos.
**Detect:** Literal string scans:

| Trigger | Wrong | Right |
|---------|-------|-------|
| its / it's | "wagged it's tail" | its = possessive; it's = it is/has |
| there / their / they're | any cross-use | place / possessive / they are |
| lets / let's | "Lets go" as suggestion | let's = let us |
| whose / who's | "who's coat" | whose = possessive; who's = who is |
| could/would/should **of** | always wrong | could/would/should **have** (of is a preposition, can't form tenses) |
| where / wear / were | any cross-use | place / clothing / past of be |
| wrong past participle | "have went," "have ate," "has did," "has came" | have gone, eaten, done, come — check the dictionary's bold forms |
| accept / except | "excepted the award" | receive / excluding |
| compliment / complement | "complimentary colors" (matching) | praise / completes-goes-with |
| affect / effect | "didn't effect our plans" | affect = verb, effect = noun (usually) |
| phase / faze | "didn't phase him" | faze = disconcert |
| led / lead | "he lead the team" (past) | led = past tense |
| then / than | "better then" | then = time; than = comparison |
| plural apostrophe | "the Garcia's," "laptop's for sale" | plain plural, no apostrophe |
| grammer | — | grammar |

**Fix:**
1. Run the trigger list over the text.
2. Apostrophe pairs: expand the contraction — if "it is/has," "they are," "let us," "who is" fits, keep the apostrophe form; otherwise its/their/lets/whose.
3. Modal + "of" → modal + "have."
4. Participles after have/has/had → the dictionary's bold participle, not the past tense.
5. affect/effect: verb → affect, noun → effect (rare senses aside). then/than: time → then, comparison → than.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The dog wagged it's tail. AFTER: The dog wagged its tail.
- BEFORE: We should of left earlier. AFTER: We should have left earlier.
- BEFORE: Liam has went to the deli twice. AFTER: Liam has gone to the deli twice.
- BEFORE: The delay didn't effect our plans. AFTER: The delay didn't affect our plans.
**Exception:** Legitimate crossovers: verb "effect" = bring about ("effect positive change"); noun "affect" = displayed emotion (psychology). One-off typos are forgivable — only the repeated iconic errors read as "I'm a rank amateur," so spend proofreading energy here first, not on perfection everywhere.

## Quick scan list

- **Stacked auxiliaries** — 2+ helpers (had been -ing) with no "another" event → simplest tense.
- **Present verbs on finished events** — completed action, present tense, or unanchored present narration → simple past.
- **Past-perfect chains** — consecutive "had + participle" sentences → anchor once, then simple past.
- **Tense drift** — mixed tenses in one timeframe ("was... who is") → conform.
- **Past-tense timeless facts** — "discovered that X was" for a still-true X → present in the that-clause.
- **Fragment check** — no subject + finite verb between periods → attach or complete (unless deliberate).
- **Subordinator-only sentence** — because/although/when clause standing alone → join to main clause.
- **Run-on / comma splice** — two independent clauses fused or joined by a bare comma → period, comma + conjunction, or subordinate.
- **Copular + -ly** — feel badly / smells terribly → adjective complement.
- **Agreement** — verb number vs. subject head noun; there's + plural; either/or; one-of-the-X-who → §5a routine.
- **Preposition + subject pronoun** — "between you and I" → object form.
- **Demand + conjugated verb / hypothetical "was"** — → subjunctive base form / were.
- **Series comma inconsistency** — both "A, B and C" and "A, B, and C" present → one policy.
- **Stacked-adjective commas** — run and-test + swap-test → add or delete commas.
- **Missing junction comma** — long intro or two joined independent clauses, no comma → insert.
- **Comma pairs** — restrictive with commas / nonrestrictive without / unclosed ", Inc." ", Jr." dates → fix pair.
- **Plural apostrophe** — 's on a word that owns/contracts nothing → delete.
- **Quote-mark order** — period/comma outside quotes, colon/semicolon inside → move; ?/! by logic.
- **Double terminal marks** — "Tex.." or "?"." → keep one.
- **Preposed compound** — ambiguous two-word modifier before noun → hyphenate; never after -ly.
- **Misplaced prepositional phrase** — phrase beside wrong noun → move next to what it modifies.
- **Iconic-error strings** — it's/could of/have went/effect-as-verb/then-than → run the full trigger table.
