# Reader-First: Macro Decisions for Sentences and Passages

Covers the Reader-serving doctrine and passage-level decisions: what each sentence is really about, information hierarchy within and across sentences, sentence-length strategy, subject–verb interruptions, and craft-vs-art calibration.
Does NOT cover: grammar mechanics of subordination/passives/nominalizations (structure.md), modifier placement (modifiers.md), or word-level fat-cutting (concision.md).

Governing test for every rule below: "The Reader is king. You are his servant." A sentence works only if the Reader — not the writer — gets value from it.

Examples note: BEFORE/AFTER pairs model a writer revising their own text, so some AFTERs sharpen details the writer knows. When editing someone else's text, any AFTER that adds or narrows facts beyond the BEFORE requires source support or an `[AUTHOR CHECK]` — the no-new-facts invariant in SKILL.md outranks every rule here.

---

### Serve the Reader, Not Yourself

**Rule:** Before finalizing any sentence, answer "What am I really trying to say?" and "Why should the Reader care?" Cut or reframe any content that exists only to serve the writer.

**Detect:**
- Emotion-soliciting self-reference ("no one understood how much I suffered")
- Unexplained insider names, in-jokes, or references treated as familiar
- Jargon-dense noun phrases a stranger can't parse
- Verdict words telling the Reader what to conclude (shockingly, sadly, outrageous)
- Accumulated detail with no stated consequence for the Reader

**Fix:**
1. State in one line what the sentence is really trying to say.
2. State in one line why the Reader should care.
3. Rewrite so that answer is the sentence's overt content.
4. Add the context a stranger needs for any insider reference, or cut it.
5. Replace verdict words with facts the Reader can judge for himself.
6. Content aimed at the wrong audience for the document type (a private budget aside in an all-staff memo, legal boilerplate in a feature story): quarantine it under a clear transition or recommend the cut — either way, flag `[AUTHOR CHECK: does this belong in this document?]`.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Nobody at that school ever understood how much I was hurting, and I deserved so much better from all of them. AFTER: That year I learned how much a fourteen-year-old can lose and still show up for class. (Stops demanding pity; offers a theme the Reader can connect to.)
- BEFORE: Deb and Rico stopped by again — classic Deb and Rico, you know how they get. AFTER: My neighbors Deb and Rico stopped by — the couple who bicker over whose turn it is to drive. (Insider reference given the context a stranger needs.)

**Exception:** Diaries, journals, and diary-style blogs where the Reader is a willing voyeur may legitimately be writer-serving. You can't serve all Readers all the time — but all Readers want clear, concise sentences that mean something to them.

### Lead With Reader Impact

**Rule:** Make the consequence for the Reader the main subject and verb — not the institution's routine action or the writer's own process.

**Detect:** Main clause of the shape [institution/official body] + [routine process verb: voted, met, approved, announced, released, discussed], with the human consequence buried in a trailing that/which clause or prepositional phrase. Applies equally to news leads, business memos, and ad copy.

**Fix:**
1. Find the fact that actually changes the Reader's life.
2. Ask: how does this affect the Reader? Why should he care?
3. Promote that fact to main subject and main verb.
4. Demote the procedural action (the vote, the announcement) to a later sentence or drop it.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The school board on Monday voted to adopt a calendar that eliminates the weeklong February break. AFTER: Students won't be getting their February week off next year. (The consequence, not the routine vote, becomes the main clause.)
- BEFORE: Management on Friday announced a policy ending remote work on Fridays. AFTER: Your remote Fridays are ending, management announced. (Same move outside journalism: the announcement is the snoozer; the effect is the story. Note the AFTER asserts only what the BEFORE contains — no invented start date or schedule.)

**Exception:** The procedural fact can stay when it is itself what matters to this Reader. Don't overcorrect into manufactured drama — see No Sleaze Bait.

### Main Point in the Main Clause

**Rule:** Rank the sentence's facts by importance to the Reader; give the single most important one the main clause, with a concrete actor as subject and an action verb. Everything else reads as accessory to the main clause.

**Detect:**
- Main clause reports something trivial while pivotal actions sit in stacked participial or subordinate phrases ("After X-ing, Y-ing, and Z-ing, ...")
- Abstract or -ing subject where a concrete actor belongs
- Sentence opens with a long subordinate wind-up before reaching the point

**Fix:**
1. List every event or fact in the sentence.
2. Rank them by importance to the Reader.
3. Rewrite so the top-ranked item is the main clause with a concrete subject and strong verb.
4. Move remaining items to their own sentences or clearly subordinate positions. (Subordination/nominalization mechanics: structure.md.)

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: After walking into the lab, grabbing the vial, injecting the serum, and realizing she had to flee, Dana noticed she was thirsty. AFTER: Dana walked into the lab, grabbed the vial, and injected the serum. Then she knew she had to flee. (Pivotal actions get main clauses instead of accessorizing a trivial one.)
- BEFORE: Scheduling the festival over Labor Day positively impacts attendance. AFTER: The Labor Day dates draw bigger festival crowds. (Concrete subject and informative verb take the main slot.)

**Exception:** A short subordinate opener is fine when the main clause still carries the point ("While the storm raged, the crew slept."). It fails only when the wind-up signals "stay put — it could be a while before I get to the point."

### Uncram: Decompose, Reorder, Rebuild

**Rule:** When one sentence carries many distinct facts, strip its conjunctions and fillers, list every proposition, cut what the Reader can live without, reorder into causal/chronological order, then rebuild by deliberate choice.

**Detect:**
- 30+ words, or three or more finite clauses chained by that/which/who/because/after/since
- Subordinate opener + long main clause + tacked-on ", which is..."
- Three or more prepositional phrases sharing one head word, or the same preposition repeated (over... over... over...)
- Stacked time elements on one verb; events narrated out of chronological order
- The sentence resists restatement as a list of discrete propositions

**Fix:**
1. Drop conjunctions, fillers, and connecting devices; write each idea on its own line as a standalone short sentence.
2. Delete any fact the Reader can live without.
3. Reorder the survivors until cause precedes effect and events run in sequence (the dominoes step).
4. Write one short sentence per group; the most important fact gets its own main clause and, in news, comes first.
5. Reconnect with simple transitions (so, but, then).
6. Restore specifics the breakup dropped (now, dates, ages).
7. Try more than one arrangement — there is no single correct split.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: While the book fair is predictably mobbed on weekends, holding it over Presidents Day for the second consecutive year positively impacts visitor flow over the final weekend, which is traditionally the busiest. AFTER: The book fair gets mobbed on weekends. So this year, for the second time, it will run over the long Presidents Day weekend. That relieves crowding on the busy final weekend. (Seven crammed facts pruned and split into three ordered sentences.)
- BEFORE: After being passed over as principal at Lincoln High after Ruth Vega retired and after not being hired at Adams Middle School, Dale Kim is moving to the district office, sources say. AFTER: Dale Kim is moving to the district office, sources say. He was passed over at Lincoln High after Ruth Vega retired. Adams Middle School didn't hire him either. (Main point first, backstory in clear sequence.)

**Exception:** Relative clauses are legitimate for squeezing in extra information — when the sentence isn't already overloaded. First drafts legitimately come out long and rambling: uncram in revision, not while drafting. (Repairing individual time-element attachment: modifiers.md.)

### Brevity Is a Tool — Mix Lengths by Genre

**Rule:** Master the short sentence even if you prefer long ones. Keep a long sentence only if you could write the short version and are choosing the long one deliberately. In craft writing (content-first: business letters, press releases, nonfiction, genre novels), make many sentences short but mix in longer ones; in art writing (form-first), any length that serves the Reader is allowed.

**Detect:**
- Craft prose averaging above ~25 words per sentence: too little brevity
- Runs of four or more consecutive same-shape sentences under ~8 words: drone
- A long sentence the writer cannot decompose into discrete short ideas: bumbling, not choice

**Fix:**
1. Classify the piece: craft (content over form) or art (form and beauty).
2. Craft: decompose habitual long sentences (see Uncram); verify no fact was lost.
3. Scan for monotonous runs of short sentences; recombine some to vary rhythm.
4. Art: permit any length — even 89 words — provided the decomposed version exists and the long form is a choice.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Renters read and hear all the time that it's not always enough simply to have good credit because, if other qualified applicants are pursuing and competing for the same unit, standing out is critical. AFTER: Renters hear it all the time: It's not always enough to have good credit. You need to stand out from the competition. (One overloaded sentence becomes three clear ideas.)
- BEFORE: He opened the shop. He counted the drawer. He unlocked the door. He swept the floor. He waited. Nobody came. AFTER: He opened the shop, counted the drawer, and unlocked the door. He swept the floor and waited. Nobody came. (Drone fixed by mixing lengths; the final short sentence keeps its punch.)

**Exception:** In art, all bets are off — McEwan's and McCarthy's sprawl earns its keep, and "It's a mess, but it's supposed to be a mess." Short sentences and fragments can be art too (Hemingway, The Road). The mandate is mastery of the short form, not exclusive use.

### Don't Bury the Verb

**Rule:** Keep the subject and its main verb close together; move bulky relative clauses and appositives out from between them unless the interruption demonstrably serves the sentence.

**Detect:**
- More than ~8–10 words between the subject's head noun and the main finite verb
- The shape: Subject, who/that [relative clause], [appositive], which [relative clause], VERB
- A long adverbial opener ("In addition to...") stacked onto an already busy sentence

**Fix:**
1. Locate the main verb, then its subject.
2. Mark everything wedged between them.
3. Move the interrupting material into its own preceding or following sentence, or shift it after the verb.
4. Reinstate the interruption only if it bolsters the point or creates a deliberate, attention-commanding effect.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Marla Chen, who anchors Nightline, the ABC program that has revived her career and done nothing to lift her spirits, has the stiff, careful walk of someone nursing a pulled muscle. AFTER: Marla Chen has the stiff, careful walk of someone nursing a pulled muscle. (Save the backstory for sentence two.)
- BEFORE: In addition to helping him with the practical side of relocating, Marcus, the branch manager, who was always quick with a joke and a word of advice, helped Theo face his doubts. AFTER: Marcus, the branch manager, helped Theo with the practical side of relocating. Always quick with a joke, he also helped Theo face his doubts. (Overloaded opener plus 15-word interruption redistributed into two sentences.)

**Exception:** A deliberate interruption that enacts its meaning (a 32-word insert performing the tempest it describes) or a confident, juicy convention-defying insert can stand. Don't sanitize such choices to appease sentence-length sticklers.

### Placement Counts: No Monster Openers

**Rule:** Don't open a piece, chapter, or section with a sprawling or convention-defying sentence; place long, mood-driven sentences only on a foundation of plain declarative sentences already laid.

**Detect:** First sentence of a document/section exceeds ~35 words, inverts normal structure, buries its subject or verb, or is a fragment/run-on — before any plain sentences have established a foundation.

**Fix:**
1. Isolate the opening sentence.
2. If it sprawls, rewrite as one or two clear sentences — or relocate the ornate version deeper into the piece.
3. Confirm remaining long, unconventional sentences sit amid stretches of simple ones.
4. Judge by the Reader: would he keep reading past it?

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The mural—for which Dana had mixed the paints, built the scaffold, and primed the wall—was finished by her in a two-day frenzy, skipping two meals. AFTER: Dana finished the mural in a two-day frenzy, skipping two meals. She had mixed the paints, built the scaffold, and primed the wall herself. (Lead clear; save ornate sprawl for later.)
- BEFORE (as an opener): At dusk the riders came down out of the north with their faces chalked and their long hair plaited and each of them armed for war which was their life. AFTER: Keep the sentence — but move it mid-story, atop straightforward sentences, where it works as seasoning. (The same sentence as an opener risks losing the Reader before sentence two.)

**Exception:** A confident writer or publication may gamble on a defiant long opener precisely to shake up the Reader — legitimate when the inserted material bolsters the point.

### Let Facts Stand Alone

**Rule:** When strong statements are chained with explanatory linkers, delete the linkers and write each fact as its own short declarative sentence.

**Detect:** Emphatic or emotional declaratives fused by even though / because / although / since, especially first-person statements where each clause could stand alone; a defensive, pleading, overexplaining tone.

**Fix:**
1. Identify each proposition in the chain.
2. Delete the linking subordinators.
3. Write each proposition as its own short declarative, keeping the original order.
4. Reread for force; restore a connector only if a needed causal relationship is genuinely lost.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: I fired her even though I didn't want to because she left me no choice. AFTER: I fired her. I didn't want to. She left me no choice. (Stripped bare, the same facts stand unmitigated and strong.)
- BEFORE: I quit the team even though I loved it because they lied to me. AFTER: I quit the team. I loved it. They lied to me.

**Exception:** This is for moments demanding blunt force. In prose that needs flow, connectives carrying real causal information can stay — many respected writers would keep the linked version.

### No Sleaze Bait

**Rule:** When adding Reader appeal, ask "What's important to my Reader?" — never just "What will get his attention?" State the real stakes instead of manufacturing fear or mystery.

**Detect:**
- Withheld referents: something, this one thing, what happened next
- Threat inflation aimed at family/home/money ("wants to kill your children")
- Cliffhanger deferrals: "details at eleven," "you won't believe"

**Fix:**
1. Name the thing being withheld.
2. Replace the manufactured threat with the true, ideally quantified, stakes.
3. Keep the Reader-relevant angle (per Lead With Reader Impact) but delete the teaser mechanics.
4. Verify the sentence gives information the Reader can act on rather than commanding an emotion.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Something in your garage could burn your house down tonight! Details at eleven. AFTER: Overloaded extension cords start hundreds of garage fires a year. Here's how to check yours. (Names the withheld referent; gives usable stakes.)
- BEFORE: Your retirement account is in more danger than you know! AFTER: A new fund fee will cost typical 401(k) savers about $200 a year.

**Exception:** Sleazy teasers do work as attention-getters (a broadcast staple); the objection is craft, not effectiveness. Aim for the balance between snoozer and sleazy — Reader interest earned with true importance.

### Say It Like You'd Speak It

**Rule:** Draft each sentence as you would say it aloud to a co-worker, then polish. Reject any phrasing you would never actually speak.

**Detect:**
- Front-loaded participial wind-ups ("Before considering whether to...")
- Self-conscious formality no one would say aloud
- Generic category nouns where a specific thing belongs (cola beverage, seating area)

**Fix:**
1. Say the sentence's content out loud as if telling a friend.
2. Transcribe what you said, keeping the spoken word order and everyday verbs.
3. Restore only the precision the genre genuinely requires. (Specific word substitutions: concision.md.)

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Before considering whether to madly rush the stage, I purchased and thirstily consumed a cola beverage. AFTER: Before I decided whether to rush the stage, I bought a Coke and chugged it. (The watercooler version: no wind-up, specific noun.)
- BEFORE: Upon arriving at the venue and effectuating entry, we proceeded to locate our designated seating. AFTER: We got there, went in, and found our seats.

**Exception:** Writing is not literally speech — some genres demand precision or formality speech lacks. The spoken version is the clarity baseline, not the endpoint.

### Verify Accuracy and Voice After Every Rewrite

**Rule:** After every clarity rewrite, confirm no fact, time marker, event sequence, or deliberate stylistic effect was lost or altered; restore what the breakup dropped. Every rewrite risks lost meaning, lost information, or factual error.

**Detect:** Compare rewrite against original for: dropped time markers (now, since college); event order flipped after removing before/when; changed actors (who did what); deleted qualifiers or facts; flattened stylistic devices the author placed deliberately.

**Fix:**
1. List the original's actors, actions, sequence, timing, and qualifiers.
2. Check each appears in the rewrite or was deliberately, consciously omitted.
3. Restore chronology that removed subordinators had been carrying (add then, dates, ages).
4. Identify effects the original structure created (frenzy, mood, rhythm); preserve them unless accidental.
5. Re-verify factual accuracy of every reworded claim.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: He finally decided it was time to take the plunge. AFTER: Now, at fifty-one, he decided it was time to take the plunge. (The breakup lost that the decision was happening now; restore it.)
- BEFORE: He took the carpentry course at the community center. He took a welding class. AFTER: He took a welding class. Then he took the carpentry course at the community center. (Removing "before"/"when" scrambled the chronology; restore the sequence with "then.")

**Exception:** If the "voice" being protected is bumbling ineptitude rather than choice — the writer's mental Cuisinart, not McEwan's tempest — rewrite freely. The safeguard protects deliberate effects, not accidents.

### Kill Meta-Announcements and Statements of the Obvious

**Rule:** Delete sentences and clauses that announce what you are about to say, restate what the surrounding text already shows, or state what any Reader infers unaided.

**Detect:**
- Announcement frames: "There were two options." / "Let me explain what I mean." / "That being said," / "It's worth noting that" — followed by the content itself
- A clause restating what an adjacent clause already established ("I was conflicted. Part of me wanted to stay and part of me didn't.")
- Emotional or logical conclusions the facts already force ("...and that made me sad")

**Fix:**
1. Delete the announcement; keep only the content it introduced.
2. For each clause, ask: does the surrounding text already show this? If yes, cut.
3. Reread — if the passage still makes its point, the cut stands.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: I had two options. I could keep quiet or I could confront him. I decided the best choice was clear: I should confront him. AFTER: I should confront him. (The options and the deciding are implied.)
- BEFORE: It's worth noting that the deadline moved to Friday. AFTER: The deadline moved to Friday.

**Exception:** A genuine signpost in long technical or instructional writing ("Three factors matter here:") can serve the Reader as navigation. Cut it only when the content arrives immediately anyway.

### Cut Redundant Clauses and Doubled Actions

**Rule:** When two coordinated verbs or clauses describe one event, keep the verb that carries the information and cut the other.

**Detect:** Verb pairs where one implies the other: "met with X and told him," "cancel the membership and end all involvement," "reached out and contacted," "sat down and wrote"; clause pairs where the second restates the first in different words.

**Fix:**
1. Ask: do the two verbs name two separate events the Reader needs?
2. If one event, keep the more informative verb; fold in any unique detail from the cut verb.
3. Reread for lost sequence; restore a genuine second event if you find one.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The CEO met with the IT director and told him the response was too slow. AFTER: The CEO told the IT director the response was too slow. (The telling implies the meeting.)
- BEFORE: She canceled her membership and ended all involvement with the gym. AFTER: She canceled her gym membership.

**Exception:** Keep both verbs when they narrate genuinely distinct actions ("He signed the contract and faxed it back") or when the doubling is deliberate rhythm.

### Explain Fully or Not at All

**Rule:** For every explanation, give the Reader the actual specifics or delete the explanation entirely — never gesture at a reason you don't deliver.

**Detect:** Vague explanation stubs: "we're just too different," "for various reasons," "things didn't work out," "due to circumstances"; a "because" clause whose content is a category, not a fact.

**Fix:**
1. Ask: what are the actual specifics behind this stub?
2. If the source (or the author) can supply them, write them.
3. If not, delete the stub — the bare statement is stronger than a half-explanation.
4. In an edit for someone else, flag with `[AUTHOR CHECK]` instead of inventing specifics.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: I knew I should dump him. We were just too different. AFTER: I knew I should dump him. He wanted a quiet life in his hometown; I had a one-way ticket to Prague. (Or, if the specifics are unavailable: I knew I should dump him.)
- BEFORE: The launch was delayed for various reasons. AFTER: The launch was delayed. (Or name the reasons.)

**Exception:** Mystery held back deliberately for later payoff (fiction) is a device, not a stub — verify the payoff exists.

### Cut Modifiers That Serve the Writer, Not the Reader

**Rule:** Delete modifiers and word choices whose purpose is the writer's ego, timidity, or self-protection rather than the Reader's understanding — and when a bold word is right, commit to it.

**Detect:** Show-off vocabulary where a plain word serves; coy self-censorship ("@!#$," "you-know-what"); intensity added to protect a weak claim ("very unique opportunity"); qualifiers softening a statement the writer should own ("sort of," "arguably," "in my humble opinion").

**Fix:**
1. Ask of each modifier: who is this for — the Reader's picture, or the writer's image?
2. Cut writer-serving modifiers outright.
3. If self-censorship hides a word the passage needs, either commit to the real word or restructure so no placeholder is needed. Never silently change a charged word choice in someone else's text — flag `[AUTHOR CHECK]`.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: I did something everyone knows you shouldn't do — that being I fell for a @!#$ friend. AFTER: I fell for a friend. (If you're going to swear, swear; if not, don't wink at it.)
- BEFORE: This is, in my humble opinion, arguably the most effective option. AFTER: This is the most effective option.

**Exception:** Full-strength profanity, slang, or flourish that is genuine voice stays — the target is reflexive oomph-seeking and coyness, not personality.

### Pare-Down Modifier Test

**Rule:** Test every suspicious modifier by reading subject + modifier + verb as a literal claim; if the literal claim is not what the writer means, recast or cut. Allow each action at most one time element.

**Detect:** Adverb + adjective pairs that don't literally parse ("predictably crowded" — is predictability a way of being crowded?); the same preposition heading multiple phrases in one sentence (over... over... over); two or more time elements attached to one verb ("on Tuesday... last week").

**Fix:**
1. Read the modifier literally against what it modifies; state what the writer actually means.
2. Recast so the sentence says that plainly ("so crowded you could predict it" → "consistently mobbed").
3. Give each verb one time element; move extra time facts to their own clauses or sentences.

**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The show is predictably crowded over the weekends. AFTER: The show gets mobbed every weekend. (The manner reading of "predictably" isn't the meaning.)
- BEFORE: The board on Friday approved the plan after debating it last month. AFTER: The board approved the plan Friday. It had debated the plan for a month.

**Exception:** Sentence adverbs legitimately modify whole claims ("Predictably, the show was crowded") — reposition rather than delete when that's the intended meaning.

## Quick scan list

- **Serve the Reader:** self-pity, insider refs, jargon, verdict words → answer "really saying?" / "why care?"
- **Lead With Reader Impact:** [institution] + voted/announced/approved with consequence in trailing clause
- **Main Point in Main Clause:** trivial main clause; pivotal action in stacked participial/subordinate phrases
- **Uncram:** 30+ words, 3+ chained clauses, tacked-on ", which...", repeated prepositions, stacked time elements
- **Brevity/Mix:** craft avg >~25 words/sentence; or 4+ same-shape short sentences droning
- **Don't Bury the Verb:** >~8–10 words between subject and main verb
- **No Monster Openers:** first sentence of piece/section >~35 words or convention-defying
- **Let Facts Stand Alone:** emphatic declaratives chained by even though/because; pleading tone
- **No Sleaze Bait:** withheld referents, threat inflation, "details at eleven"
- **Say It Like You'd Speak It:** phrasing that fails the read-aloud test; participial wind-ups
- **Kill Meta-Announcements:** "There were two options." / "It's worth noting that" / restated conclusions
- **Cut Doubled Actions:** "met with X and told him"; verb pairs naming one event
- **Explain Fully or Not at All:** "too different," "various reasons," category-not-fact because-clauses
- **Cut Writer-Serving Modifiers:** show-off words, coy self-censorship, "in my humble opinion" hedges
- **Pare-Down Modifier Test:** literal read of modifier + target; >1 time element per action; repeated prepositions
- **Verify Accuracy and Voice:** run after every rewrite — dropped time markers, flipped sequence, flattened deliberate effects
