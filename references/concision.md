# Concision and Word Choice

Covers cutting fat and replacing vague words with concrete ones: opaque nouns/verbs, hedged frames, dead-weight modifiers, filler phrases, failed metaphors, fact-crammed sentences, and Reader-cost decisions on semicolons, parentheses, and quote attribution.
Master test for every rule: does the choice serve the Reader or the writer's ego? Goal is economy of words — maximum information per word — not fewer words. A 134-word sentence can be tight; an 8-word one can be all lard.
NOT covered here: nominalizations and passives (structure.md); punctuation rules reference (grammar-mechanics.md).
Examples note: BEFORE/AFTER pairs model a writer revising their own text (fiction examples especially — "decide it now" is the writer's power, not the editor's). When editing someone else's text, any AFTER that adds or narrows facts requires source support or an `[AUTHOR CHECK]` (SKILL.md invariant).

### Specific words over opaque ones — or cut the detail

**Rule:** Replace every opaque generic noun and verb with the most specific word the facts support; if the specific fact is unknowable, delete the detail entirely — never hedge with "something."
**Detect:** Trigger nouns: person, man/woman (unmodified), thing(s), item(s), structure, place, area, noise, effect, procedure, get-together, repairs, solutions. Generic verbs: moved, went, took, got, had, implemented, occurred, enjoyed, utilize(d), impact(ed), affect(ed), conduct(ed), address(ed). Hedged indefinites: something, some kind of, of some sort, a bite of something.
**Fix:**
1. Flag each opaque noun, generic verb, and hedge in the sentence.
2. Ask what it specifically refers to. Fiction: decide it now (that's the writer's job). Nonfiction: check notes/sources.
3. Substitute the most specific word or short phrase the facts support.
4. If the fact is unknowable, delete the mention — you don't have to report every detail.
5. Stop before overload: one or two concrete details per sentence usually suffice.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The person was moving through the place carrying the things. AFTER: The escaped convict sprinted down the cereal aisle clutching a mop handle and the manager's key ring. (Every opaque word swapped; the sentence becomes a scene.)
- BEFORE: The mayor took a bite of something as we talked. AFTER: The mayor bit into a glazed cruller as we talked. (If you noticed it well enough to mention it, name it.)
- BEFORE: The ambassador nibbled at something during the interview. AFTER: The ambassador answered every question without pausing. (Unknowable detail cut, not hedged.)
- BEFORE: Lisbeth took her car in for some repairs. AFTER: Lisbeth drove her Prius to Campbell's Toyota for new brake pads. (Modest specificity suffices — no need to max out.)
**Exception:** Sometimes no better word exists — the answer can be no. Not every sentence needs packed detail; too much description backfires. Omission is always allowed; the rule governs only details you choose to report.

### Make bold statements

**Rule:** Replace hedged meta-frames ("one of the more remarkable aspects of X is the fact that...") with a direct main clause stating the fact itself; the Reader decides what's remarkable.
**Detect:** Main clause is meta-commentary while substance hides in a that-clause: "one of the more/most [adj] aspects/features/things about X is (the fact) that..."; "X has adopted a [noun] that is..."; hedges: decidedly, somewhat, arguably; comparative tails: "than that of their counterparts"; main verb is/are governing abstract nouns (aspect, feature, quality).
**Fix:**
1. Locate the concrete information — usually inside a that-clause or after "aspect"/"that is."
2. Promote it: make its subject and verb the main clause.
3. Delete the meta-frame and hedges.
4. State the claim flatly; the Reader knows a generalization is debatable — no disclaimers.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: One of the more remarkable aspects of the campus is the fact that it has nine historic libraries. AFTER: The campus has nine historic libraries. (Six words beat eighteen.)
- BEFORE: Parisians have adopted a lifestyle that is decidedly more leisurely and stylish than that of their fellows in the provinces. AFTER: The Parisian lifestyle is leisurely and stylish.
- BEFORE: One of the most notable features of the phone is the fact that it charges in ten minutes. AFTER: The phone charges in ten minutes.
**Exception:** Chopping softer qualifying tails is more subjective — keep hedging content that carries genuinely new information. Standard is economy of words, not minimum word count.
**Hedge calibration:** boldness applies to the *frame*, not the claim's epistemic strength. When the source itself hedges the claim ("it is expected that X will improve"), the rewrite keeps an equivalent modal ("X should improve") — stripping the frame must not upgrade a prediction into a promise.

### Collapse "he is a man who" frames

**Rule:** In "X is a [category noun] who/that/which + news," delete the copula and category noun and promote the relative clause to main clause.
**Detect:** Subject + form of "to be" + generic same-referent category noun (man, woman, person, place, thing, activity, house, one) + relative pronoun. Shapes: "He is a man who...", "X is a place that...", "-ing is an activity that...", "X's is a house that...".
**Fix:**
1. Match the pattern (the category noun refers to the same thing as the subject).
2. Delete copula + category noun; make the relative clause's verb the main verb.
3. If the sentence equates subject with a description, make the real information the predicate.
4. Confirm the news now has top billing in the main clause.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Elena is a woman who works very hard. AFTER: Elena works hard. (The intensifier goes too.)
- BEFORE: Denver is a place that gets snow. AFTER: Denver gets snow.
- BEFORE: Swimming is an activity that amounts to good exercise. AFTER: Swimming is good exercise.
- BEFORE: Mia's is a house that was built in the 1920s. AFTER: Mia's house was built in the 1920s.
**Exception:** None given — always correct this construction.

### Chop flabby insertions and filler frames

**Rule:** Cut filler and segue phrases or replace each with its shortest equivalent (also; for X; whether; because); these clusters work in speech but waste ink.
**Detect:** "In addition to..." (especially with a later "also" — the redundancy voids the whole phrase); "for his/her/X's part"; "in terms of"; "the question as to whether"; "is a subject which"; "used for ... purposes"; "as if this weren't enough"; "considering all that"; "due to the fact that"; "the fact that"; "in order to" (→ to); "prior to" (→ before); "at this point in time" (→ now); "is able to / has the ability to" (→ can); "it is important to note that / it should be noted that" (→ cut); subjective purpose-tails ("to help employees cultivate work/life balance").
**Fix:**
1. Delete the phrase and reread; if meaning survives, keep the deletion.
2. If a segue is needed, use one word: "also"; "for Brady's part" → "for Brady"; "the question as to whether" → "whether."
3. Replace every "due to the fact that" with "because" — no exceptions.
4. For remaining "the fact that": recast as "Because X, Y" or promote the buried verb; try the bare that-clause as subject.
5. Question subjective tails; chop if they add nothing.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: In addition to tuition reimbursement, Acme Corp. also offers dental coverage and gym stipends. AFTER: Acme Corp. also offers dental coverage and gym stipends. ("Also" already does the segue.)
- BEFORE: The question as to whether the merger will close is a subject which worries investors. AFTER: Whether the merger will close worries investors.
- BEFORE: Due to the fact that Nina lies, nobody trusts her. AFTER: Because Nina lies, nobody trusts her.
- BEFORE: That Marla cheats means she's dishonest. AFTER: The fact that Marla cheats means she's dishonest. (Last-resort exception: "the fact" marks an abstract that-clause as a subject when every recast is awkward.)
**Exception:** Not every "in addition to" is bad — fine when it pairs genuinely new items rather than re-summarizing. "The fact that" may stay as a last resort; "due to the fact that" never.

### Cut dead-weight adjectives

**Rule:** Delete attributive adjectives that assert value judgments (renowned, brilliant, awesome) or stack 3+ before one noun; show the trait later with concrete evidence instead.
**Detect:** Opinion adjective before a name or role noun (renowned, legendary, brilliant, eccentric, remarkable); 3+ stacked adjectives; a character's first mention carrying a trait the text never demonstrates.
**Fix:**
1. Flag every attributive adjective.
2. Classify: fact (verifiable — bloodied, limping, vaulted) vs. value judgment (renowned, brilliant).
3. Delete value judgments; if the trait matters, demonstrate it later with action or detail.
4. Keep a fact adjective only if it adds a picture the noun lacks.
5. If 3+ stack, keep at most the single most informative one (less is more — stripped-down lands harder).
6. Leave predicate adjectives ("Aunt Greta was tall") alone.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Renowned architect Marta Ellis hurried through the vaulted atrium. AFTER: Marta Ellis hurried through the atrium. (Cut "renowned"; wait for a chance to show her renown.)
- BEFORE: The big, terrifying, vicious, totally out-of-control escaped tiger ran toward me. AFTER: The escaped tiger ran toward me.
- BEFORE: Awesome and brilliant surgeon Dana Reyes entered the operating room. AFTER: Bloodied and limping surgeon Dana Reyes entered the operating room. (Fact adjectives beat value judgments; better still, show traits later.)
**Exception:** Adjectives aren't bad per se. Predicate adjectives work perfectly; fact adjectives that build a mental picture are justified; borderline calls (vaulted) are judgment weighing what they add.

### Delete goldbricking adverbs

**Rule:** Delete adverbs whose meaning the verb already contains, bare intensifiers, and try-hard impact adverbs — unless one adds information the sentence otherwise lacks. "When in doubt, take them out." (This list generates candidates only — the deletion test in `modifiers.md` is the single authority on every keep/cut decision.)
**Detect:** -ly adverb duplicating its verb/participle (freshly renovated, first begin, already existing, currently working); red-flag intensifiers: very, extremely, really, incredibly, unbelievably, astonishingly, totally, truly, currently, presently, formerly, previously; impact manner adverbs: cruelly, happily, wantonly, angrily, sexily, menacingly, blissfully; adverb + action verb where the verb implies the manner (quickly grabbed).
**Fix:**
1. Flag every adverb, especially -ly forms.
2. If the verb/participle or tense already implies it, delete.
3. Delete bare intensifiers from the red-flag list.
4. For impact adverbs, read the sentence without it; keep only if it supplies information the action doesn't show.
5. Keep whichever version emphasizes substance.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The freshly renovated $40-million resort hearkens to Miami's glory days. AFTER: The renovated $40-million resort hearkens to Miami's glory days. (As meaningful as "freshly refreshed.")
- BEFORE: He very cruelly mocked the extremely nervous intern. AFTER: He mocked the nervous intern. ("Cruelly" dies here because mocking already implies it; contrast "Senator Vane laughed cruelly" in modifiers.md, where the adverb changes the laugh's meaning — the deletion test decides, not the word list.)
- BEFORE: Marcus quickly grabbed a wrench. AFTER: Marcus grabbed a wrench. (The bare verb conveys immediacy better.)
**Exception:** Red flags, not banned words — they appear in the best writing too. Occasionally "quickly" is crucial information; reasonable editors can disagree on a given cut.

### Delete definition-level redundancy and empty modifiers

**Rule:** Delete any word whose meaning is already entailed by a neighboring word's definition; keep the element carrying more information, and replace circular self-defining descriptions with concrete specifics.
**Detect:** Modifier entailed by its noun — negation test: if "nonambient weather" or "inessential essence" is incoherent, the modifier is empty. Overlapping pairs: "known to be" + "notoriously"; "the exact same"; "famously renowned." Circular descriptions: "an essence blended from essential oils and ingredients."
**Fix:**
1. State what the strong word's definition already asserts.
2. Scan for a neighbor repeating part of that assertion.
3. Delete the weaker element; keep the richer word (don't auto-cut the adverb).
4. Replace circular words with substance (name the actual oils, features, ingredients).
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Spring is a great time to enjoy the valley's ambient climate. AFTER: Spring is a great time to enjoy the valley's climate. (A valley's climate is by definition ambient.)
- BEFORE: Startup valuations are known to be notoriously volatile. AFTER: Startup valuations are notoriously volatile. ("Notoriously" already means famously-and-badly; cut the flabbier partner.)
- BEFORE: Both labs reached the exact same conclusion. AFTER: Both labs reached the same conclusion. (Pounding the point weakens it.)
- BEFORE: Each elixir, a blend of pure botanical extracts with natural ingredients, is inspired by the elements. AFTER: Each elixir blends lavender oil, chamomile, and sea kelp.
**Exception:** Keep a seemingly redundant word that pulls extra weight (notoriously) and cut its partner instead. Rare marketing contexts (spa copy) may keep soft empty words as a knowing trade-off when the honest substance would repel the Reader.

### Unpack fact-stuffed sentences

**Rule:** Move facts out of parentheses and off chained attribution participles into their own full sentences; serve information linear, one bite at a time — cramming is force-feeding.
**Detect:** Parentheticals containing numerals, prices, measurements, dates, model names, specs; any parenthetical over 5 words; 2+ parentheticals in one sentence. Attribution pattern: [quote] + said + comma + -ing phrase (suggesting/adding/noting/implying) with a long complement, especially chained with "and"; attribution tail longer than the quote.
**Fix:**
1. Extract the content of each parenthetical or participial phrase.
2. Smooth the host sentence after deletion.
3. Recast each extracted fact as its own clause or sentence with a real subject and finite verb.
4. Reconnect with a transition (and, yet, but) where needed; order the new sentences logically.
5. Keep at most one tiny parenthetical or one short, natural modifier of "said."
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Though it's more tablet (the base model weighs 1.9 pounds) than laptop (fewer ports than the cheaper Z3), the X1 comes close to the hype. AFTER: It's more tablet than laptop. The base model weighs 1.9 pounds and offers fewer ports than the cheaper Z3. Yet the X1 comes close to the hype.
- BEFORE: "Our renovated resort will be better than ever," Karen Diaz said, suggesting guests try the rooftop pool and adding that the cafe is now open 24 hours. AFTER: "Our renovated resort will be better than ever," Karen Diaz said. She suggested guests try the rooftop pool. The cafe is now open 24 hours as well.
- BEFORE: The landlord promised (he has promised repairs three times since March, most recently in June) to fix the heater. AFTER: The landlord promised (again) to fix the heater. (Shrink the aside to pure voice; move needed facts to their own sentence.)
**Exception:** Tiny parentheticals — "The trail ($5 entry fee) starts at dawn" — can be the best way to serve information. Short voice asides (~5 words or fewer of attitude, not data: "(yeah, right)") serve the Reader; leave them intact — unpacking flattens the wink. A single brief "said, adding that..." is legitimate. Long parenthetical mazes can delight when a stylist builds them for the Reader, not for the writer's convenience.

### Cash in failed metaphors

**Rule:** If a metaphor, comparison, or turn of phrase doesn't enhance the Reader's experience, replace it with plain language stating what literally happened. Kill your darlings.
**Detect:** Inanimate/abstract subjects performing agentive organic actions (the sun stewed, grief fermented); stacked figurative verbs in consecutive sentences; imagery raising unanswerable literal questions about what physically occurred; a phrase kept after multiple readers flagged it (author attachment). Stacking threshold: two or more figurative images modifying one event ("towering column of flame" + "cathedral of destruction") triggers trim-to-the-strongest or an `[AUTHOR CHECK]` — one event, one image, unless the pile-up is a confirmed fingerprint device.
**Fix:**
1. Ask: does this figure enhance the story or only please the writer?
2. If it doesn't enhance, write the plain-fact version in clear words.
3. Preserve the story-relevant information (who, what, where, when).
4. Accept losing beloved imagery.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: The moon had simmered all evening for that midnight. At first dark it leered hungrily on Marco's village. AFTER: By midnight, moonlight flooded Marco's village. (A moon can't simmer or leer.)
- BEFORE: Her grief distilled into the afternoon, where it slowly began to ferment. AFTER: Her grief deepened as the afternoon wore on. (Plain language keeps the story moving.)
**Exception:** Metaphors that sharpen the scene ("The fire breathed when the wind breathed...") or stand on their own as art stay. These edits are subjective; creative writing isn't bound by logic — Reader-serving writing only requires that plain alternatives be considered.

### Anchor the mushy core

**Rule:** When the subject-verb core is vague or misused ("a monotone shirred"), introduce a concrete noun and a plainly understood verb; demote the vague word to an appositive or delete it.
**Detect:** Subject is an abstract quality or sound-word with no concrete head noun (a monotone, a timbre, a drone). Verb is rare, misused, or ambiguous between common senses (shirred; drew = attracted or sketched?). Test: paraphrase subject+verb alone — if it conveys nothing checkable, the core is mush.
**Fix:**
1. Determine what concretely happened (a sound occurred; shadows appeared).
2. Bring in a concrete noun (sound, noise, stain).
3. Keep the vague word, if worth keeping, as an appositive restating the concrete noun.
4. Choose a verb whose common meaning fits exactly (came, appeared, rose).
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: A sullen timbre purled from the cellar. AFTER: A strange sound—a sullen hum—came from the cellar. (Concrete noun anchors; the vague word survives as appositive.)
- BEFORE: The corridor drew long shadows as dusk became one with the plaster. AFTER: At dusk, long shadows appeared along the corridor's plaster walls. ("Drew" is ambiguous; "appeared" states what happened.)
**Exception:** One odd-but-evocative word amid concrete prose can survive; it must go only when the passage is composed almost exclusively of vague words. Claimed intentional ambiguity does not excuse a core that doesn't work.

### Audit literal meaning: nonsense assertions and runaway phrases

**Rule:** Reread each sentence for what it literally says; rewrite self-contradicting assertions and pet phrases that invert who does what to whom, stating the intended point in plain subject-verb form.
**Detect:** (a) "You needn't/don't have to X to Y" where Y is definitionally accomplished by X (do the math → figure out the number; count heads → know the count). (b) Genitive "the [deverbal noun] of [NP]" (the reach of, the draw of, the pull of) where the NP is the object of the hidden verb, not the possessor. Test: paraphrase the clause literally and check whether the claim negates itself or assigns the action to the wrong party.
**Fix:**
1. State in one plain sentence what the writer actually meant.
2. Identify the absurd clause, or the verb hiding inside the noun and its true agent/object.
3. Rewrite so the sentence asserts the point directly, agent as subject, verb active.
4. Verify the rewrite preserves the original meaning.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: You don't need a calculator to compute the total cost of eight nights of gifts. AFTER: You don't need a calculator to see how quickly the costs mount. (Computing totals is exactly what calculators do.)
- BEFORE: The podcast raises the stakes by having the reach of a national audience. AFTER: The podcast raises the stakes because it can reach a national audience. (The audience is the reachee, not the reacher.)
- BEFORE: There's no need to count heads to figure out the number of guests. AFTER: There's no need to count heads to see the party has outgrown the patio.
**Exception:** Intentional irony/humor where the absurdity is the point. True possession genitives stand: "the ear of the senator," "the brains of Einstein." Never change meaning while removing nonsense.

### No invented compound modifiers

**Rule:** Replace made-up hyphenated compounds ("illness-fated girl," "failure-doomed man") with an established word or the standard construction they compress — even if plainer.
**Detect:** Hyphenated noun+participle attributives absent from standard usage: X-fated, X-doomed, X-bound, X-destined. Test: unpack it ("doomed to failure") — if the unpacked form is idiomatic but the compound isn't, it's invented.
**Fix:**
1. Unpack the compound into its underlying phrase.
2. Search for an established single word covering the idea (sickly, unlucky).
3. Use it even if cliched — cliche beats invention.
4. Otherwise rewrite predicatively with the standard construction.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Tomás was a misfortune-fated boy. AFTER: Tomás had always been an unlucky boy. (Plain, even cliched — far better than an invented compound.)
- BEFORE: He was a failure-doomed entrepreneur. AFTER: The entrepreneur was doomed to failure.
- BEFORE: She played the ruin-bound heiress with relish. AFTER: She played the heiress headed for ruin with relish.
**Exception:** The underlying idea is worth keeping — only the compressed rendering must go. Compounds already established in the language are fine.

### Name the whole, not the part

**Rule:** When a description names a component of an object that already contains it, replace the part-plus-whole phrase with the whole object.
**Detect:** "the [component] on/of a [modified whole]" where the whole's modifier already names the component (frosting on a frosted cake, topping on a candied apple) — especially inside similes: "as X as the [part] on a [whole]."
**Fix:**
1. Identify the whole object being partially described.
2. Confirm the part is already implied by the whole.
3. Replace the entire phrase with the whole object.
4. Verify the sentence's appeal still works applied to the whole.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: It's as enticing as the fudge coating on a fudge-dipped cone. AFTER: It's as enticing as a fudge-dipped cone. (If the coating entices, the cone entices.)
- BEFORE: It was as bracing as the salt on the rim of a salted margarita. AFTER: It was as bracing as a margarita.
**Exception:** If the part alone is genuinely the appeal and the whole is not (loves caramel, hates apples), the whole-object comparison fails — choose a different comparison entirely rather than restoring the part-of-whole phrasing.

### Discipline from...to constructions

**Rule:** Keep from...to constructions short, comma-free, and unnested; abandon and recast once a leg nests its own from/to, semicolons appear, or the modifier delays the main clause.
**Detect:** "from ... to ..." phrases, especially sentence-initial or as "everything from ... to ..." subjects; legs longer than a few words; a "from"/"to" nested inside a leg; semicolons holding it together; errant commas before "to" legs (the construction takes none).
**Fix:**
1. Locate each from...to construction; measure its length.
2. Remove commas between "from" and its "to" legs.
3. If a leg nests its own from/to or needs semicolons, abandon the construction — recast as a plain list or separate sentences.
4. If it's a long introductory modifier, lead with the main clause or shorten the legs.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: From donating a few hours weekly to nearby shelters to joining charity runs to raise research funds, millions of us donate our time. AFTER: Millions of us donate our time to myriad causes, from weekly hours at shelters to charity runs. (Nested "to" made a mess; main clause now leads.)
- BEFORE: Everything from what software will be needed to how customers will book and pay needs to be built. AFTER: The team still needs to build everything: the software, the booking flow, the payment system.
- BEFORE: From soup made with the finest ingredients, to nuts we hand dip, you'll love our service. AFTER: From soup made with the finest ingredients to nuts we hand dip, you'll love our service. (Comma-free — though needing commas signals the setup isn't cutting it.)
**Exception:** Nothing wrong with from...to per se — short ones ("from soup to nuts") work fine. The advice is not to lean on it, and to bail once it gets unwieldy.

### Quoted words are verbatim

**Rule:** Never rewrite text inside quotation marks — a quote is a fact about what someone said. When flab, redundancy, or vagueness lives inside a quote, choose: (a) leave the quote intact and note the issue; (b) trim to a partial quote, keeping only the words worth quoting ("fully committed" rather than the whole sentence); or (c) paraphrase outside quotation marks and drop the quote — never a silent in-quote edit.
**Detect:** Any planned edit whose span crosses a quotation mark; quotes containing detection-list tokens (redundancy, intensifiers).
**Fix:**
1. Confirm the offending words are inside quotation marks.
2. Prefer (b) partial quote when a phrase is genuinely worth quoting; (c) paraphrase when nothing is; (a) leave-and-note when the quote's full wording is the point or the speaker's phrasing is newsworthy.
3. Never let attribution trimming change what the speaker is on record as saying.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Okafor said the council was "fully committed to a total resolution." AFTER: Okafor said the council was "fully committed" to resolving the crisis. (Partial quote sheds the in-quote redundancy without touching the speaker's words.)
- BEFORE: "We are actively exploring all available options at this time," Cho said. AFTER: The company is weighing its options, Cho said. (Nothing quote-worthy — paraphrase.)
**Exception:** Fiction dialogue belongs to the author, not a real speaker — it may be edited like any prose, subject to the voice guard.

### Said is the default attribution

**Rule:** Attribute quotations with "said"; replace showy verbs (enthused, extolled, opined); choose any alternative only with great care and only when it conveys genuine, accurate extra information. In LIGHT EDIT, a kept nonstandard attribution verb (intoned, purred) that isn't genre-typical gets an `[AUTHOR CHECK]` rather than a silent pass.
**Detect:** Attribution verbs other than said: enthused, extolled, opined, chuckled, smiled, gushed; transitive misuse ("enthused" a quotation — you enthuse over something); "echoed the sentiment" — red flag that quotes are being strung together instead of written.
**Fix:**
1. Scan every attribution verb.
2. Replace show-off substitutes and dictionary misuses with "said."
3. Keep a nonstandard verb only if genre allows it and it accurately conveys emotion or volume.
4. On "echoed the sentiment," rewrite the passage so quotations are woven into actual writing, not chained.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: "The lineup is all new," Alvarez enthused. AFTER: "The lineup is all new," Alvarez said. (Enthuse isn't transitive; the fancy verb serves the writer.)
- BEFORE: "Test scores are climbing," Principal Reyes extolled. AFTER: "Test scores are climbing," Principal Reyes said.
- BEFORE: "Get out of my house," she hissed. AFTER: "Get out of my house," she hissed. (Fiction exception — genuinely conveys emotion.)
**Exception:** Genre calibration: journalism — said is a virtue, simple and unadorned; change every frilly one. Fiction — hissed, screamed, hollered, moaned, explained, replied can work ("laughed" divides editors), but it's elbow room, not carte blanche. Get creative because it works, not to show off.

### Semicolons earn their keep

**Rule:** Use semicolons only as ubercommas between list items that contain internal commas; replace clause-joining semicolons with a period (or a comma before a conjunction), and break up semicolon-stuffed sentences.
**Detect:** Semicolon whose two sides each have subject + finite verb; "; and" / "; but"; 3+ semicolons in one sentence; two semicolon-lists joined by a conjunction; an identical element (same state, same title) repeated in every item; semicolons between items that have no internal commas.
**Fix:**
1. Lists: if items contain internal commas (Austin, Texas), put semicolons BETWEEN items, commas inside. If not, plain commas only.
2. Delete elements repeated in every item; if internal commas disappear, revert semicolons to commas.
3. If the sentence still carries two lists or clauses, split at the conjunction into separate sentences.
4. Clause-joining semicolon: replace with a period and capitalize; before a coordinating conjunction, use a comma or nothing.
**Examples:**
- BEFORE: Dana visited Austin, Texas, Reno, Nevada, Tampa, Florida, and Boise, Idaho. AFTER: Dana visited Austin, Texas; Reno, Nevada; Tampa, Florida; and Boise, Idaho. (Reader-serving: each pair reads as one unit.)
- BEFORE: She hoped to tour Dallas, Texas; Austin, Texas; and Waco, Texas, and had invited her uncle, Raj; her neighbor, Kim; and a beagle. AFTER: She hoped to tour Dallas, Austin, and Waco. She had invited her uncle, Raj, her neighbor, Kim, and a beagle. (Delete the repeated state; split the sentence; zero semicolons.)
- BEFORE: The garden was thriving; the tomatoes were ripe by June. AFTER: The garden was thriving. The tomatoes were ripe by June. (Clause-joining semicolons are "often justified, but never necessary.")
- BEFORE: "Now rinse; and your hair will feel like satin," she said. AFTER: "Now rinse, and your hair will feel like satin," she said.
**Exception:** Ubercomma semicolons are crucial when items truly need internal commas and the sentence stays short. If the writer deliberately links two clauses as one thought, an editor leaves that voice choice alone — but as writer, prefer separate sentences; a semicolon-riddled page is hard on the Reader's eyes.

## Quick scan list

- **Specific over opaque:** person / thing(s) / item(s) / structure / place / noise / procedure / get-together / something / some kind of; generic verbs (moved, went, got, implemented)
- **Bold statements:** "one of the more [adj] aspects of X is (the fact) that..."; "has adopted a [noun] that is"; decidedly / arguably
- **He-is-a-man-who:** X + be + man/woman/place/thing/activity + who/that/which
- **Flabby insertions:** in addition to...also; for X's part; in terms of; the question as to whether; due to the fact that; the fact that; in order to; prior to; at this point in time; is able to; it is important to note that
- **Dead-weight adjectives:** renowned / brilliant / awesome before a name; 3+ stacked adjectives
- **Goldbricking adverbs:** very / extremely / totally / currently / already; -ly duplicating the verb (freshly renovated, quickly grabbed); menacingly / cruelly
- **Definition-level redundancy:** modifier entailed by noun (ambient climate); overlap pairs (known to be notoriously, the exact same); circular descriptions
- **Fact-cramming:** parentheticals with numerals/specs or >5 words; 2+ per sentence; "said, suggesting... and adding..."
- **Quoted words are verbatim:** flab inside quotation marks → partial quote, paraphrase, or leave-and-note; never edit in-quote
- **Failed metaphors:** inanimate subjects doing organic actions; stacked figurative verbs; 2+ images on one event; unanswerable literal questions
- **Mushy core:** abstract subject + rare/misused/ambiguous verb (a monotone shirred; drew stains)
- **Literal-meaning audit:** "you needn't X to Y" where X = Y; "the reach/draw/pull of [object-not-possessor]"
- **Invented compounds:** unhyphenated-in-the-wild X-fated / X-doomed / X-bound attributives
- **Part-of-whole:** "the [component] on/of a [whole that includes it]" in comparisons
- **From...to:** legs over a few words; nested from/to; commas or semicolons inside; long intro modifier
- **Attribution:** any quote verb other than said (enthused, extolled); "echoed the sentiment"
- **Semicolons:** joins two independent clauses; "; and"; 3+ per sentence; list items lacking internal commas
